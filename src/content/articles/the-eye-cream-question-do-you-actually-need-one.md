I have spent about €1,400 on eye creams in the last ten years. That is more than I have spent on face moisturizers, serums, and facial cleansers combined. The eye cream is the single most expensive product category in my routine, by total spend, and the category with the least convincing evidence for what it actually does.

This is not a hit piece on eye cream. Some eye creams do real things. The question I want to address is what an eye cream actually does, what it does not, and when the answer is that you do not need one at all. After ten years of buying the expensive ones, the cheap ones, the gimmick ones, and the dermatologist-recommended ones, here is what I have learned.

## What an eye cream actually is

Most eye creams are moisturizers. The formulation is similar to a face moisturizer — water, humectants (glycerin, hyaluronic acid), emollients (squalane, shea butter), some active ingredients, preservatives. The differences from a face moisturizer are mostly packaging and marketing.

The real formulation differences are usually:

**Lower concentration of active ingredients.** Eye creams are formulated to be gentler than face moisturizers because the skin around the eye is thinner and more reactive. The active ingredients (retinol, vitamin C, peptides) are present at lower doses than in the equivalent face product. This is appropriate for sensitive eye-area skin, but it also means the results are slower and more modest.

**More emollient, less occlusive.** Eye creams tend to be richer than face moisturizers in texture but less occlusive in formulation. They are designed to sit on the surface of the skin without migrating into the eye. This is a real formulation choice, not a marketing claim.

**Smaller packaging.** The smaller jar is partly a formulation necessity (air exposure degrades some actives faster) and partly a pricing strategy. A 15ml eye cream at €60 costs €4 per ml. A 50ml face moisturizer at €40 costs €0.80 per ml. The eye cream is five times more expensive per unit of product.

## What an eye cream actually does

The honest answer is: it moisturizes the skin around the eye. The active ingredients, if present at meaningful concentrations, can do one or more of the following.

**Hydrate.** Hyaluronic acid, glycerin, and squalane-based eye creams hydrate the skin. The hydration is real, but a face moisturizer does the same thing.

**Plump fine lines temporarily.** Hyaluronic acid and certain peptides can plump the skin temporarily, making fine lines less visible. The effect lasts hours, not days. It is a real effect, but it is a cosmetic effect, not a structural change.

**Address hyperpigmentation.** Vitamin C, kojic acid, and certain peptides can address dark circles caused by hyperpigmentation. The effect takes 6 to 12 weeks of consistent use. A face serum with the same ingredients at higher concentration will do the same thing faster.

**Address puffiness.** Caffeine-based eye creams reduce puffiness temporarily by constricting blood vessels. The effect lasts hours. It is a real effect, but it does not address the underlying cause.

**Support collagen.** Retinol-based eye creams stimulate collagen production in the long term. This is the most substantive effect an eye cream can have. The result is slower onset (3 to 6 months) and modest in magnitude, but real.

## What an eye cream does not do

The claims that eye creams make that they cannot back up.

**Erase wrinkles.** No topical product erases wrinkles. Eye creams can soften the appearance of fine lines through hydration and temporary plumping. They do not erase structural wrinkles caused by repeated facial movement or loss of collagen.

**Lift the eye area.** No topical product lifts skin. Lifting is a structural change that requires in-office treatments (microneedling, lasers, surgery) or, in modest cases, the gradual collagen remodeling of consistent retinol use over years.

**Replace sleep, hydration, or genetics.** Dark circles caused by sleep deprivation, dehydration, or genetic under-eye pigmentation do not respond to topical treatment in any meaningful way. An eye cream is the wrong category for these problems.

**Cure milia, xanthelasma, or other eye-area conditions.** These require in-office treatment.

## When you need one

The eye cream is the right category in three specific scenarios.

**You have very dry eye-area skin that does not respond to face moisturizer.** Some people have eye-area skin that is drier than the rest of their face. A richer, more emollient eye cream can address this. A face moisturizer applied around the eye is usually too light for these skin types.

**You want to use retinol around the eye and need a lower-concentration formula.** The eye area is more reactive to retinol than the rest of the face. A dedicated eye cream with retinol at lower concentration (0.025% to 0.05%) is appropriate for this area. A face retinol applied around the eye often causes irritation.

**You want to address a specific eye-area concern (dark circles from hyperpigmentation, puffiness from fluid retention).** Targeted ingredients (vitamin C for hyperpigmentation, caffeine for puffiness) in an eye cream format can address these concerns. A face product with the same ingredients is usually effective but may be too irritating for the eye area.

## When you do not need one

If none of the above apply, you probably do not need a separate eye cream. A face moisturizer applied around the eye will do the same job for less money. The skin around the eye is not a different organ. It is the same skin, slightly thinner, slightly more reactive. The same products, in many cases, work fine.

The exception is if the face product contains ingredients that are not appropriate for the eye area — high-strength retinol, strong exfoliating acids (AHA/BHA at concentrations above 10%), certain essential oils. In that case, a separate eye-area product is necessary. But that is a question of avoiding irritation, not of getting better results.

## What I have used

A brief accounting of the eye creams I have used, ranked by what they actually did.

**Best for hydration and barrier: Avène Cicalfate Eye Cream.** Around €25 for 15ml. The texture is rich without being occlusive. It sits well under makeup. It is appropriate for sensitive skin. I used it for two years and saw no dramatic results, which is the point — the eye area was healthy and hydrated. The cost-per-use was reasonable.

**Best for retinol: Roc Retinol Correxion Eye Cream.** Around €30 for 15ml. The retinol concentration is low (about 0.025%), which is appropriate for the eye area. I used it for six months and noticed a modest improvement in fine lines around the crow's feet area. The result was not dramatic but was real.

**Best for dark circles: La Roche-Posay Pigmentclar Eye Cream.** Around €35 for 15ml. The formulation includes vitamin C and caffeine. I noticed modest improvement in under-eye darkness over three months. The improvement was not enough to justify the price on its own — the effect was about the same as my regular vitamin C serum applied around the eye.

**Best for puffiness: The Ordinary Caffeine Solution 5% + EGCG.** Around €8 for 30ml. The cheapest option, the most targeted for puffiness, and the one that delivered the most visible single-application effect. The bottle lasted over a year because the formulation is light and a small amount goes a long way.

**Worst: most "premium" eye creams.** The €80+ eye creams from luxury brands are almost always less effective per dollar than the pharmacy-tested alternatives. The premium is in the packaging, the marketing, and sometimes the texture. The active ingredient profile is usually less concentrated than the cheaper alternatives.

## The simple alternative

If you do not want to buy a separate eye cream, the simplest alternative is to use your existing face products around the eye, with one caveat: avoid face products with strong actives (retinol above 0.05%, AHA/BHA above 10%) around the eye area unless they are specifically formulated for the eye.

A routine that does not include a separate eye cream:

**Morning.** Splash face with water. Apply hyaluronic acid serum to damp face, including around the eye. Apply face moisturizer, including around the eye. Apply sunscreen, including around the eye.

**Evening.** Cleanse. Apply face serum (retinol, vitamin C, peptides — whatever your routine uses), avoiding the immediate eye contour if the concentration is high. Apply face moisturizer, including around the eye. Apply a richer eye-area product only if the eye area is dry.

This routine is what I currently use. The eye cream I have on the shelf right now is half-used and will probably be replaced by a face moisturizer when it runs out. The morning and evening routines work fine without a dedicated eye product.

## What to buy if you buy one

If the answer is "I need one," the criteria I would use.

**For hydration:** A simple, fragrance-free moisturizer in eye-area-safe packaging. Avène Cicalfate, La Roche-Posay Toleriane, CeraVe Eye Cream, or a pharmacy-brand equivalent. Around €15 to €30. No need to spend more.

**For retinol:** A low-concentration retinol eye cream (0.025% to 0.05%). Roc, The Ordinary, or a similar retinol database. Around €20 to €40.

**For dark circles (hyperpigmentation):** A vitamin C-based eye cream. Skinceuticals CE Ferulic around the eye is too expensive; The Ordinary Caffeine Solution or a pharmacy vitamin C eye cream is sufficient.

**For puffiness:** The Ordinary Caffeine Solution 5%. €8. Nothing else is necessary.

The most expensive eye cream I would recommend is in the €40 range. Anything above €60 is paying for packaging, marketing, or brand prestige. The active ingredients are not materially better.

## The honest summary

Eye creams are the most expensive moisturizers in most routines, sold in the smallest jars, on the basis of the weakest evidence. They can hydrate, plump fine lines temporarily, address hyperpigmentation over time, and reduce puffiness. They cannot erase wrinkles, lift the eye area, or replace sleep.

Most people do not need a separate eye cream. A face moisturizer applied around the eye does the same job for less money. The exception is when you have specific eye-area concerns that benefit from targeted active ingredients, or when your face products are too strong for the eye area.

If you buy one, buy a low-cost, evidence-backed option. Avoid the €80+ premium formulations. The expensive ones are not materially better than the cheap ones.

The eye cream is the category where marketing has most successfully convinced people to spend the most for the least result. The honest answer is that you probably do not need one. If you want one, you do not need to spend much.

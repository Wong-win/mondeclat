I own forty-one bottles of perfume. This is too many. It is also fewer than I owned in 2019, when the count was seventy-three. The difference between then and now is not restraint. It is curation. The forty-one bottles I have now are the ones I actually wear. The seventy-three included the ones I had been talked into buying by marketing, the ones I wore once and could not bring myself to throw out, and the ones that turned out to smell different on me than they did in the shop.

This is how I built the collection, what I learned from going through a phase of buying too much, and how the rotation actually works in practice.

## The starting point: a small wardrobe

When I started wearing perfume in my twenties, my approach was to wear whatever was on the dressing table. This was usually a single bottle — whatever a previous relationship had left behind, or whatever a friend had given me for Christmas, or whatever the duty-free shop had suggested at the airport. The result was that I had one perfume. Maybe two. I wore them interchangeably.

The problem with one or two perfumes is that you stop smelling them. Olfactory fatigue is real and fast. After three days of wearing the same fragrance, you cannot smell it on yourself. The people around you can. You are the only one walking around in a cloud of something you cannot detect.

The fix is rotation. Wearing a different perfume on different days keeps all of them perceptible. This is the central insight of any perfume wardrobe — that the goal is not to find one scent but to have a small selection you cycle through.

The minimum for a working perfume wardrobe is four. The maximum, for most people, is ten. The collection I have now, at forty-one, is a collector's collection, not a working wardrobe. The working wardrobe is the ten I actually wear. The other thirty-one are bottles I keep because they mean something to me, because they fill specific niches, or because I have not been able to part with them.

## How I think about perfume categories

The perfume world organizes itself into roughly seven categories. Understanding them is the first step to building a rotation.

**Citrus.** Light, fresh, daytime, summer. Examples: Acqua di Parma Colonia, Dior Eau Sauvage, Atelier Cologne Orange Sanguine. The most "polite" category — almost never offensive, often forgettable.

**Floral.** The largest category. Ranges from single-flower soliflores (rose, jasmine, tuberose) to complex bouquets. Chanel No. 5, Dior Miss Dior, Le Labo Rose 31. The most versatile category for year-round wear.

**Green.** Cut grass, leaves, herbs. The category that smells like the outdoors without being citrusy. Chanel No. 19, Diptyque Eau de Lierre, Hermès Un Jardin Sur Le Nil. Underrated and under-marketed.

**Aquatic.** Marine, ozonic, fresh. The "clean" category. Acqua di Gio, Davidoff Cool Water, Maison Margiela Sailing Day. Polarizing — people either love them or find them sterile.

**Woody.** Sandalwood, cedar, vetiver. The most consistently warm and grounding category. Tom Ford Oud Wood, Le Labo Santal 33, Hermès Terre d'Hermès. Most perform well in colder months.

**Oriental / amber.** Resins, vanilla, spices, warm balsams. Guerlain Shalimar, Tom Ford Black Orchid, Yves Saint Laurent Opium. The most polarizing and the most distinctive. Often performs poorly in heat.

**Fougère.** The traditional masculine category. Lavender, oakmoss, coumarin. The structure of most men's classic fragrances. Dior Sauvage, Chanel Bleu de Chanel, Acqua di Parma Colonia Essenza.

A working wardrobe should cover at least four of these categories, so you have options for different moods, contexts, and seasons.

## The bottles in my rotation

A practical description of what I actually wear, organized by context.

### Daily office

**Hermès Eau de Cologne Hermès.** Light, clean, citrusy. Worn in spring and summer when I want something that fades by lunchtime and does not project beyond arm's length. The 100ml bottle is €90. Lasts about four months of daily wear.

**Maison Margiela Lazy Sunday Morning.** The clean, soapy, fresh linen scent. Worn when I want to smell like I just got out of the shower. Works year-round but particularly in warm weather. €95 for 100ml.

### Evening and going out

**Tom Ford Black Orchid.** The most distinctive scent I own. Worn when I want to be noticed. The projection is significant — people will comment. €115 for 50ml. Lasts about eight months because I do not wear it often.

**Le Labo Santal 33.** The most "cool" scent in the rotation. Woody, smoky, slightly leathery. Worn when I want to smell like I have a more interesting life than I do. €220 for 50ml.

### Cold weather and dressy

**Guerlain Shalimar.** The classic oriental. Worn in winter for evening occasions. €95 for 50ml. The bottle has lasted me five years because I do not wear it often.

**Chanel Coromandel.** The most complex oriental I own. Worn when I want something Shalimar-adjacent but more layered. €280 for 75ml.

### Signatures and personal scent memory

**Acqua di Parma Colonia.** The scent I wore in my twenties. The scent my ex-partner associates with me. The scent I wear now and then when I want to remember who I was at 25. €120 for 100ml.

**Diptyque Eau de Lierre.** A green, ivy-scented cologne. The scent I associate with a specific person I loved at 30. The reason I have kept it even though I rarely wear it. €85 for 100ml.

### The niche bottles

**Frederic Malle Portrait of a Lady.** The most expensive bottle I own (€260 for 50ml) and the one I wear least. Worn when I want to feel like the kind of person who wears €260 perfume. Probably four times a year.

**Maison Francis Kurkdjian Baccarat Rouge 540.** The most complimented scent I own. Worn when I want compliments. €240 for 70ml.

That is ten bottles in rotation. The other thirty-one are kept for specific reasons — gifts from people I love, discontinued bottles I cannot bear to part with, vintages of fragrances that have since been reformulated. They live in a box in my closet and I check on them occasionally.

## How I decide what to wear in the morning

The decision takes about thirty seconds. The variables are:

**Weather.** Citrus and aquatics in heat. Woody and oriental in cold. Floral and green in temperate weather.

**Context.** Office means light projection. Evening means stronger projection. Outdoor activities mean nothing that might fade with sweat.

**Mood.** This is the variable the marketing under-emphasizes. The right perfume for a confident mood is not the right perfume for a contemplative mood. I match the scent to the feeling I want to project.

**Day of the week.** A loose rotation. Monday, Wednesday, Friday tend to be citrus and floral. Tuesday and Thursday tend to be woody and green. This is not a rule. It is a tendency.

**Skin chemistry.** Fragrance interacts with skin chemistry. Scents that smell one way on a tester strip may smell different on your skin. The way to figure this out is to wear a fragrance for a full day before deciding. Most department stores sell decants or offer sample programs that allow this.

## The mistakes I made collecting

The collecting phase (2017 to 2022) was expensive and educational. The mistakes worth noting:

**Buying by description.** The fragrance descriptions in magazines and on websites are aspirational at best, deceptive at worst. "Smells like a summer garden in Tuscany" usually means "smells like lavender and synthetic musk." The only way to know what a fragrance smells like is to wear it.

**Buying by celebrity endorsement.** Celebrity fragrances are usually not made by people who know perfumery. They are made by marketing departments targeting brand websites.

**Buying without wearing first.** The most expensive mistake. Most department stores offer samples or decants. Use them. A €200 fragrance you do not like is worse than a €20 fragrance you do.

**Buying the larger bottle to save money.** The bigger bottle is only cheaper per ml if you finish it. If you do not finish it (and most people do not finish expensive fragrances), the bigger bottle is more expensive in absolute terms. Start with the smaller size.

**Hoarding discontinued scents.** Some discontinued fragrances have cult followings. The secondary market prices reflect this. If you want a discontinued scent, the prices are what they are. If you do not, do not pay the premium.

## How to start a perfume wardrobe

The practical advice for someone starting from scratch.

**Step one: identify two or three categories you like.** Wear a fragrance from each category. Notice which ones you actually like on your skin. Avoid citrus if you do not like citrus. Avoid orientals if you do not like orientals. The list is long. You do not need to like all of it.

**Step two: buy the smallest size of two or three you like.** Most brands sell 30ml or 50ml sizes. Buy small. Wear for at least a week each.

**Step three: add one more per year.** A working wardrobe grows slowly. Adding one new fragrance per year, on average, lets you build the collection without overspending.

**Step four: replace before you expand.** If a fragrance runs out and you want to repurchase, that is signal it is part of your wardrobe. If you never want to repurchase, give it away. The wardrobe that grows by replacement is the wardrobe that gets worn.

## The honest summary

Perfume is the category in personal style where the gap between marketing and reality is widest. The marketing wants you to believe that the right fragrance will transform your life. The reality is that fragrance is a small daily pleasure, a way to mark a day or a mood, and a quiet signal to yourself and the people around you.

The collection I have is not necessary. A working wardrobe of three or four well-chosen fragrances would do almost everything the forty-one do. The thirty-seven extras exist because I enjoy them, because they connect me to specific times and people, and because the box they live in takes up a corner of a closet.

If you are starting out, do not start with forty. Start with one or two. Add slowly. Wear them for years before replacing. The perfume that gets worn is the perfume that earns its place. Everything else is decoration.

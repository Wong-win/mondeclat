I have moved apartments three times in twenty-six months. This is not a flex. It is a confession. Each move had a reason that made sense at the time. The reasons add up to a pattern I am only now able to see clearly, which is that I did not know what I wanted from a home and kept moving to find out.

This is what the moves looked like, what they cost in money and time, and what I have learned that I wish I knew at the beginning.

## The three moves

**Move one: August 2024.** From a one-bedroom apartment in the Marais, Paris, to a one-bedroom apartment in the 11th arrondissement. The Marais apartment had been my home for three years. The reasons for leaving were mostly aesthetic — I had outgrown the neighborhood, the apartment was dark, the building had chronic heating issues. The new apartment was brighter, more modern, and €200 a month more expensive.

**Move two: March 2025.** From the 11th arrondissement apartment to a two-bedroom in the 19th. The reasons for this move were different — my partner and I had decided to cohabit, the 11th was too small for two people, and the 19th had a balcony we both wanted. The new apartment was €400 a month more expensive than the 11th. We signed a two-year lease.

**Move three: October 2025.** From the 19th arrondissement to a smaller one-bedroom in the 10th. The reasons for this move were the hardest. The relationship had ended. The two-bedroom was too big and too expensive for one person. I found a one-bedroom I could afford on my own and moved. The new apartment was €300 a month cheaper than the 19th. I signed a one-year lease.

That is three moves in twenty-six months. Each one made sense at the time. The cumulative cost — financial, emotional, and practical — was higher than I would have predicted.

## The financial cost

The financial cost of moving is the part most people underestimate. The visible costs — the movers, the deposit, the new furniture — are obvious. The hidden costs are larger.

**The movers.** Each move cost roughly €800 to €1,200 for a one or two-bedroom apartment in Paris, including packing materials. Three moves is roughly €3,000 to €3,600 in movers alone.

**The deposits.** Parisian rentals require a deposit of one to two months' rent. Each move required a new deposit while the previous one was being held. The overlap periods cost me roughly €4,500 in cash that was locked up at various points.

**The overlapping rent.** I paid overlapping rent on two moves (the Marais to 11th, and the 19th to 10th) because I could not perfectly time the move-in and move-out dates. The overlap periods were usually two to four weeks. Total overlapping rent: roughly €2,800.

**The furniture and household items.** Moving is the period when you discover what is wrong with your furniture. The cheap bookshelf that worked in one apartment does not fit through the door of the next. The mattress that was fine for one person is too soft for two. The curtains that fit the windows of the old apartment do not fit the new one. I spent roughly €3,000 across the three moves on replacements and additions.

**The lost items.** Things get lost in moves. Keys to old apartments. Small kitchen tools. The book you have been meaning to finish. The cables that go to devices you no longer own. The irreplaceable items — the family photos, the documents, the small mementos — were carefully packed. The replaceable items were casualties. Estimated value of lost items: roughly €500.

Total estimated financial cost: roughly €14,000 across the three moves. That is more than a year of rent at the original apartment. The moves cost as much as a year of living.

## The emotional cost

The financial cost is the smaller part. The emotional cost was the larger part.

**The disruption to routine.** Each move is a two- to four-week period during which nothing is settled. The kitchen is half-set up. The closets are not organized. The shower curtain is missing. The Wi-Fi is not connected. The mail is being forwarded. The small habits of daily life — where the keys go, where the tea lives, where the shoes are dropped — all need to be re-established.

I underestimated this. I had assumed that moving into a new place would be exciting, the way it was when I was twenty-five. At thirty-eight, it was exhausting. The excitement of a new place is real, but it does not compensate for the disruption of routine. The disruption is bigger than the excitement.

**The social disruption.** Moving changes the geography of friendships. The Marais apartment was a fifteen-minute walk from three close friends. The 11th was twenty minutes by metro. The 19th was forty minutes. The 10th is twenty minutes. Each move required renegotiating the geography of social life — who came over for dinner, who I saw on weeknights, who stopped by casually. The friends I was closest to survived the moves. The friends I was less close to did not.

**The identity disruption.** A home is part of identity. The first apartment I lived in on my own was a statement about who I was. The next apartment was a refinement. The third was a relationship statement. The fourth is a return to a smaller version of the first. The places I have lived have been part of the way I have described myself to other people. The instability of three moves in twenty-six months has made the descriptions harder.

**The grief.** The third move was a grief move. The relationship had ended. The apartment was a record of the relationship. Moving out was a small additional grief layered on top of the larger one. I did not expect how much the physical act of packing would hurt. The book we read together. The chair he had given me. The kitchen drawer where we had organized the spices. Each item was a small reminder that needed to be packed, moved, and put away in a new configuration.

## What I learned about what I actually want from a home

The most useful thing I learned is that I did not know what I wanted from a home. The moves were attempts to find out. The reason there were three moves instead of one is that each apartment clarified what was wrong with the previous one without clarifying what was right about the next one.

What I have figured out, finally:

**I want to live alone.** This was not clear before the third move. The first two apartments were lived in alone or with a partner. The third is the first time I have lived alone since the first move. Living alone is what I prefer. The next apartment should be designed for one person, not for a hypothetical partner.

**I want to live near work.** I underestimated how much the commute mattered. The 19th apartment was forty minutes from my office. The 10th apartment is fifteen minutes. The difference is roughly an hour a day, which is roughly two hundred fifty hours a year. Time that I now spend cooking, reading, or sleeping.

**I want light.** The Marais apartment was dark. The 11th was medium. The 19th was bright but in a way that overheated in summer. The 10th has east-facing windows, which means morning light without afternoon heat. The light matters more than I expected.

**I want a small space.** The two-bedroom in the 19th was too much space for one person. The one-bedroom in the 10th is the right size. The smaller space is easier to clean, easier to heat, easier to make feel like a home. The next apartment should be similar in size to the current one.

**I want a one-year lease.** The two-year lease on the 19th apartment was a mistake. I knew at six months that the apartment was not right, but the lease obligation kept me there for eighteen more months. The one-year lease on the 10th gives me flexibility without the risk of a difficult landlord.

## The systems I use now to make moves easier

The four moves (including the first long-term apartment) have taught me what to do to make the next one cheaper and less disruptive. The systems:

**Label everything.** Boxes labeled by room and contents take half the time to unpack at the new apartment. I learned this on move three and will never move without labels again.

**Photograph the empty apartment.** Before moving out, I photograph every wall, every outlet, every fixture. The photos are evidence of the state of the apartment when I left, which has been useful twice in getting deposits back.

**Keep a packing list.** I now keep a running packing list in my notes app. Items get added as I think of them. The list is checked during packing. The result is fewer forgotten items and fewer lost things.

**Buy the right boxes once.** I keep a stack of ten moving boxes in my cellar. They fold flat when not in use. The boxes have paid for themselves across multiple moves. The cost of buying new boxes for each move adds up.

**Have a "first night" bag.** The first night in the new apartment, I do not unpack. I have a bag with sheets, towels, toiletries, a change of clothes, and a phone charger. The bag lets me sleep in the new apartment without the pressure of unpacking. The unpacking happens over the next week.

## How to avoid needing to move

The real lesson is that the moves themselves are a symptom of not knowing what I wanted. The system that prevents future moves is the system that figures out what I want before signing a lease.

The questions I now ask before signing any lease:

**Will I be happy here in two years?** Not just on day one, but in two years. The apartment that is exciting on the viewing can be miserable after the first winter.

**Is this apartment large enough for the life I have, not the life I imagine?** Most moves are caused by apartments that were right for the imagined life and wrong for the actual life. The 19th apartment was right for the imagined life of a couple. It was wrong for the actual life of one person.

**Can I afford this on my current income, without a partner?** The most resilient lease is the one that I could afford if my circumstances changed. The lease that requires two incomes to maintain is the lease that becomes untenable when circumstances change.

**Is this near enough to the things I care about?** The geography matters. The apartment near friends, near work, near the things I do most often, is the apartment that will continue to work as my life evolves.

The questions do not guarantee that I will not move again. They reduce the probability. The next move, if it happens, should be because the answers to these questions changed, not because I did not ask them.

## The honest summary

Three moves in twenty-six months is too many. The pattern of moving was the symptom. The cause was not knowing what I wanted. The fix is not a system. The fix is the clarity that the moves were looking for.

The current apartment is the right one for now. I have been here for ten months. I am not planning to move. The lease is up in December, and I am planning to renew. This is the longest I have stayed in one place since the original Marais apartment.

The lesson, if there is one, is that moving is not the answer to not knowing what you want. Moving is a way of postponing the question. The question will come back, and you will move again, and the cost will be paid in money and time and the disruption to the things that matter.

If you are considering a move, ask first what the move is for. If the answer is vague, the move will not help. If the answer is specific, the move might.

Most of life is this. The art is in the questions you ask before the action.

There is a particular kind of quiet embarrassment that comes from looking at the work selfies you took in 2019 and realizing you cannot believe anyone took you seriously. I keep a folder of those photos, and I look at them sometimes the way you look at your parents' wedding album — affectionately, with distance, grateful for the memories but absolutely certain you would not repeat the choices.

My office wardrobe in 2026 is the result of six years of editing. It contains forty-one pieces, hangs in one rail and one drawer, and gets me through every working day of the year without thinking. That last part is the actual point. The goal was never to look good. The goal was to stop thinking about clothes at 7:30 in the morning so my brain could think about something else.

What follows is not aspirational. It is descriptive. These are the pieces I actually wear, in the combinations I actually wear them, with the prices I actually paid and the ones I would pay again.

## The rails

The rail is the spine of the system. Everything in it has survived at least one cull. Most of it has survived several.

The blazers: three. A navy hopsack from Suitsupply, bought in 2021 on sale for €380, altered at the waist for €45. A charcoal flannel from Boglioli, second-hand on Vestiaire for €220, slightly wide in the shoulders in a way I have come to like. A cream linen from Massimo Alba, full retail at €890 and worth every cent because the lining is bemberg and the buttons are horn and the construction is the kind of thing you can see with your eyes closed.

The trousers: four. Two pairs of pleated wool in grey and navy. One pair of chinos in stone. One pair of heavier-weight black wool for funerals and the rare formal meeting. I have not owned jeans to the office since 2022 and I do not miss them.

The shirts: six. All white. Three in poplin, two in oxford, one in a heavier twill that I wear under sweaters. The poplins are from Charvet (two, bought in Paris when I was there for other reasons) and one from a small Jermyn Street shop whose name I have lost. The oxfords are both from Brooks Brothers and the twill is from a Suit Supply outlet for €60.

The knitwear: four sweaters. A navy Shetland from Johnstons of Elgin, two lambswool crewnecks from N.Peal that I rotate through the week, and one cashmere from The Elder Statesman that I bought in 2019 and which is now pilled beyond saving but still feels like a hug.

## The drawer

The drawer is where the personality goes. Three ties (a navy knit, a grey wool, a striped seersucker for summer). Six pocket squares (white linen, navy silk, a printed one from Drake's that I bought for a wedding). One belt in black leather, one in brown, both 30mm wide, both from J.Crew years ago.

Underwear and socks are Uniqlo and Falke respectively. The Uniqlo AIRism line is the only underwear innovation of my adult life worth endorsing. The Falke socks last three times longer than anything else I have tried.

A watch that cost more than I would admit to most people, and a second one that cost €40 from a shop in Lisbon that I wear when I do not want to think about the first one.

## The shoes

I own seven pairs of shoes for the office. That sounds like a lot until you remember that most of them are not for the office.

The work shoes: a pair of black wholecuts from Berluti (bought second-hand for €600, retailed €1,400), a pair of brown derbies from Edward Green (retail, painful, no regrets), a pair of black loafers from JM Weston (second-hand), and a pair of chunky Chelsea boots from Common Projects that I wear in winter.

The not-work shoes: white Common Projects Achilles (weekends), a pair of New Balance 990v6 in grey (walking), and running shoes that I rotate every six months.

The Berluti wholecuts are the shoes I get the most compliments on, and they are also the most uncomfortable. This is not a coincidence. The shoes that look like nothing are the shoes people notice.

## What I wear on a typical Tuesday

In winter: navy hopsack blazer, grey pleated trousers, white poplin shirt, navy Shetland sweater over the shirt (no tie), brown derbies, navy knit tie if there is a meeting, no tie if there is not.

In summer: cream linen blazer (the Alba one), stone chinos, white linen shirt, loafers, no socks in months where the temperature reliably clears 22 degrees. I work in a country where this is roughly June to mid-September, and in those months the Alba blazer gets worn probably four days out of five.

There is no particular outfit that I dread on Sunday night, and that is the test. Anything that requires more than ninety seconds of thought on a workday morning has failed.

## What got donated

The list of what did not survive is longer than the list of what did. Two suits from when I was younger and thought suits were the point. A drawer of novelty ties. Three sport coats in colors I never wore. An entire category of "smart casual" that turned out to mean "no clear answer to where you are supposed to be."

The hardest donations were the things I had spent real money on. The Brunello Cucinelli sport coat I bought in 2018 and wore exactly four times. The Kiton tie from a trip to Naples. The Loro Piana cashmere I loved for two seasons and then realized only worked with one pair of trousers. Each of those cost more than the entire current rail. None of them earned its space.

The lesson was not that expensive clothes are bad. The lesson was that expensive clothes for a life I was not actually living are worse than cheap clothes for a life I am.

## What I would buy again at three times the price

The Boglioli flannel blazer. The Charvet poplins. The Elder Statesman cashmere. The Berluti wholecuts. The Edward Green derbies.

That is five things. Everything else I would buy again at the price I paid, which means the value was adequate but not life-changing.

The five on that list are the ones where I have done the mental arithmetic of how many wears they have given me and how many I expect them to give me, and the cost-per-wear has fallen to a number that would embarrass me to say out loud. The Charvet poplin at €220 new, worn once a week for an expected ten years, costs €0.42 per wear. The Edward Green derbies at €1,500, worn three times a week for an expected ten years, are €1.00 per wear. These are numbers you can live with.

## What I would not buy again at any price

The novelty ties. The novelty socks. The bracelet I bought in Marrakech that fell apart in a year. The "investment watch" that I bought in 2017 and that has lost more money than my entire wardrobe is worth. The Suitsupply trousers that fit perfectly in the shop and fell apart at the seat after eight months.

The category, more than the items, is what I have stopped buying. Anything I cannot explain the use case for at the moment of purchase does not get purchased. This rule has saved me roughly four thousand euros a year. It has cost me nothing.

## What I would tell my younger self

Buy one less suit. Buy one better sweater. Never buy a tie because it is on sale. Never buy shoes that need to be "broken in" — they should be comfortable in the shop. Replace the word "investment" with "cost per wear" when you are tempted by anything over €500. The "investment" framing is how luxury marketing gets you to defend purchases you cannot actually afford.

And: the goal is not to have an interesting wardrobe. The goal is to have a wardrobe that does not require your attention on the mornings you do not have attention to give it.

## What my colleagues actually notice

I used to assume that the people I work with were paying attention to what I wore. They were not. The questions I have been asked about my clothes, in roughly six years of this wardrobe, are limited. A colleague once asked if my Berluti wholecuts were comfortable (they are not). Another asked where I had my trousers altered (a small tailor in the Marais). A third noticed I was wearing the same navy blazer for the third week and asked if I owned more than one (I own three).

That is it. Three questions across six years. The people who work with me have noticed almost nothing about what I wear, which is exactly the point. The wardrobe I have built is invisible in the way that a well-designed building is invisible. The structure is doing its job. The people inside the structure do not think about it.

This is the test of any working wardrobe — not how it looks, but whether anyone notices it. A wardrobe that gets noticed is a wardrobe that is doing too much. A wardrobe that does not get noticed is a wardrobe that is doing exactly enough.

## A note on changing bodies

The single hardest variable in any wardrobe is the body underneath. My body has changed in the last decade in ways I did not fully appreciate when I started the wardrobe. I have put on about four kilograms since I was thirty. The waist measurement of my trousers has gone up by about two centimetres. The shoulders have not changed. The chest has not changed. The arms have slightly thickened.

The result is that some of my older trousers no longer fit the way they did when I bought them. The Boglioli blazer from 2021 has been let out at the waist twice. The Charvet shirts have not needed adjustment. The Charvet poplins were always cut for a slightly relaxed fit, which gives them more tolerance for weight changes than most dress shirts.

The lesson is to buy pieces that can be adjusted. Trousers with extra fabric at the waist can be let out. Blazers with centre-back vents can be taken in or let out. Shirts with mother-of-pearl buttons can be replaced as the collar wears. The wardrobe that adapts to the body is the wardrobe that lasts.

The wardrobe that does not adapt — the wardrobe built on a body that no longer exists — is the wardrobe that ends up in a donation pile, along with everything else I got rid of in the years this system has been in place.

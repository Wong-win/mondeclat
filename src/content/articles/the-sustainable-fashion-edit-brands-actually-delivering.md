The phrase "sustainable fashion" has been stretched so thin by marketing that it now covers everything from a t-shirt made of recycled bottles to a six-thousand-euro coat that has not changed its production line in twenty years. Both can technically be true. Both are marketed as the same thing.

What follows is a shorter, more honest list. These are brands that publish their supply chain, pay their workers enough that workers stay, and make clothing that survives more than a season. They are not perfect. None of them is. But they are delivering.

## The new guard

### A.BCH

Made-to-order cotton basics, full transparency on every fiber, factories in Portugal and Turkey. Lead times are real — two to four weeks — which is the point. If you want it tomorrow, you are not the customer.

### Another Tomorrow

A capsule built on the same ten pieces in different fabrics. Designed for circularity: take-back program, repair credit, transparent cost-per-wear on every product page.

### Sheep Inc.

Carbon-negative merino knitwear, each piece traceable to the specific farm. Yes, your sweater has a passport. The wool comes from New Zealand, the knits are made in Portugal, and the price reflects both.

### Vestige

Womenswear out of Lithuania, deadstock fabrics, no seasonal collections. They drop when they have something worth saying. The result is small, considered, and expensive — but you are paying for the model, not the markup.

### Asket

Swedish basics brand that has refused to run a sale in a decade. Their "permanent collection" argument is real: a t-shirt costs the same today as it did in 2015. The accounting is public.

## The old guard

These are not new brands. They have been quietly making clothes under the sustainability threshold for years, before the marketing apparatus.

- **Patagonia** — The original. Repair culture, take-back program, founder gave the company away. Still the bar.
- **Eileen Fisher** — Take-back program that has been running since 2009. Renovation, resale, recycling. Boring in the best way.
- **Nudie Jeans** — Free repairs for life, organic cotton, transparent pricing.
- **Veja** — Trainers with publicly disclosed factory margins. Everyone has heard of them, almost no one knows they publish the exact wage paid to each worker.
- **Muji** — Not technically marketed as sustainable, but functionally so: minimal design, no marketing-driven obsolescence, decades-long product runs.

## What sustainability actually costs

A real sustainable sweater costs between 200 and 500 euros. A sustainable pair of jeans, between 180 and 350. A sustainable wool coat, 700 to 2500. These are not aspirational prices for most people — they are out of reach.

The honest answer is that sustainability in fashion is currently a middle-class privilege. The way to vote with your wallet, if your wallet can only stretch to fast fashion, is to buy less of it and wear it longer. A five-year-old polyester shirt that is still in rotation is more sustainable than a brand-new organic cotton one that gets donated after eight months.

## Three rules of thumb

1. **Repair culture beats material.** A garment you patch is greener than a garment you replace, regardless of fiber.
2. **Transparency is not optional.** If a brand cannot tell you where a garment was made, the answer is probably bad.
3. **Buy once.** The most sustainable purchase is the second one you do not make.

The industry is changing slowly. The brands above are proof it can change. The rest is up to what we choose to put in our carts and what we leave on it.

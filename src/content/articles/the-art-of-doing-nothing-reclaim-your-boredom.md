The average adult checks their phone every seven minutes. The average workday includes eleven micro-breaks, almost all of them digital. The average evening "relaxation" involves three streaming platforms, four social feeds, and a podcast queue that grows faster than it shrinks. The result is that people are exhausted in a way that sleep does not fix.

What has been eliminated, almost entirely, is boredom. And boredom, it turns out, is the precondition for the kind of thinking that does not happen while you are scrolling.

## The case for boredom

The 2019 study that gave the issue its modern framing came from the University of Virginia. Participants were asked to spend six to fifteen minutes alone with their thoughts, with nothing to do. Most reported finding it unpleasant. A significant minority chose to administer a mild electric shock rather than sit quietly.

That is the cultural baseline: people would rather feel pain than sit with their own thoughts. The corollary, which is more useful, is that the people who could tolerate boredom performed significantly better on creative-thinking tasks immediately afterward.

Boredom is not emptiness. It is the state in which the brain, lacking external input, starts producing its own. The thoughts that show up in those gaps are the ones that solve problems, generate ideas, and process difficult feelings. Remove the gaps and you remove the capacity.

## How boredom disappeared

Three things converged.

**Smartphones** made portable entertainment frictionless. There is no longer a moment in a day when there is literally nothing to do. Even standing in line, even waiting for a friend, even being lost — every gap used to be a small thinking moment. Every gap is now a feed.

**Streaming** made choice easier than attention. The marginal cost of starting a new show is zero. The marginal cost of finishing one is the entire evening. Most evenings end not because you chose to stop watching but because you fell asleep with the screen on.

**Productivity culture** reframed idle time as wasted time. The modern anxiety about "unoptimized" hours has made people treat a quiet Sunday afternoon as a personal failing.

The combined effect is a population that is constantly stimulated and chronically under-rested.

## What boredom actually does

A short list, from psychology and neuroscience research:

- It activates the default mode network, the brain system responsible for autobiographical memory, future planning, and self-reflection
- It is the necessary precondition for insight — the "aha" moment almost never happens during focused work, only during the unfocused drift that follows
- It restores attentional capacity, which focused work depletes
- It allows emotional processing that constant distraction interrupts

None of this requires a meditation retreat or a digital sabbath. It requires small pockets of unscheduled time, which the modern week has largely erased.

## Practical boring

The good news is that reclaiming boredom does not require grand gestures. It requires protecting small gaps.

**The gap between activities.** Most people fill the seven minutes between finishing one task and starting another. Don't. Sit. Look out the window. Let the previous task settle. The next task will start better.

**The walk without input.** No podcast, no music, no audiobook. Just walking. The research on this is consistent. Twenty minutes of walking without input is more restorative than an hour of walking with a podcast.

**The waiting room.** Doctors, mechanics, airports, queues — every waiting room used to be a tiny pocket of forced boredom. Now it is phone time. Reclaim at least some of those moments by leaving the phone in your pocket for the first five minutes.

**The morning gap.** The first twenty minutes after waking. Most people fill it with email and social. The replacement is coffee and a window. The thinking that happens in those minutes often sets the tone for the day.

**The evening gap.** The ninety minutes before sleep. Screens shut down melatonin production and the content is more memorable than the day. The replacement is a book, a journal, a conversation, or — most radical of all — nothing.

## What this is not

This is not a recommendation to be unproductive. The hours you spend in deep work are valuable. The argument is that the hours you spend in shallow input are not the recovery you think they are. They are a different kind of depletion.

It is also not a recommendation to never be entertained. Entertainment is part of a full life. The problem is the constant low-grade stimulation that crowds out the thinking space.

The goal is not more discipline. The goal is a less anxious relationship with empty time. Five minutes a day, deliberately unprotected, is enough to start.

The people who have the most interesting ideas are usually the people who have the most unprotected time to think in. Boredom, in that sense, is the engine of originality. We have nearly destroyed it. It is recoverable.

## What I have learned from doing nothing

I deleted Twitter for the sixth time in 2026. The deletion came with a deliberate commitment to protect unprotected time. The first week was strange. The second week was easier. By the fourth week, I had stopped reaching for the phone in every gap.

The thinking that came back was not dramatic. It was not the "aha" moment of a breakthrough insight. It was a quieter return of mental continuity. The thoughts that had been interrupted by every notification could now finish themselves.

The specific things I noticed:

**Problem-solving returned.** I had a work problem that had been nagging for two weeks. I had been waiting for a moment of focus to address it. The moment of focus never came during work hours because work hours were too fragmented. The problem solved itself in fifteen minutes during a Sunday morning walk with no phone.

**Creative ideas returned.** I had stopped having ideas. The realization was gradual. I was consuming more than I was producing, and the gap was closing the productive side. The first weeks of doing nothing produced a small backlog of half-formed ideas. The backlog became real work.

**Emotional processing returned.** I had been carrying a small grief about a relationship that had ended earlier in the year. The grief had not been processed because there had been no quiet time to process it. The quiet time allowed the grief to settle. The settling was not pleasant. The settling was necessary.

**Attention span returned.** The first week of doing nothing was difficult because I could not sit for more than five minutes without reaching for a phone. The second week, the maximum gap was fifteen minutes. By the fourth week, I could sit for forty-five minutes with nothing but my thoughts.

The compounding effect of the recovery is real. The thinking that happens in unprotected time is different from the thinking that happens in protected time. The protected thinking is focused, productive, and useful. The unprotected thinking is generative, slow, and unpredictable. Both kinds are necessary. The unprotected kind has been crowded out.

## The structural reasons boredom disappeared

The disappearance of boredom was not an accident. It was engineered. The forces that engineered it are worth naming, because the fix is to recognize them.

**The attention economy runs on engagement.** Every platform that captures your attention is monetizing your time. The longer the session, the more revenue. The design choices — infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications, default-on recommendations — are designed to maximize session length. The design choices work. They have eliminated the natural gaps in the day.

**Productivity culture pathologizes rest.** The modern discourse about productivity treats any unoptimized time as wasted time. The unproductivity anxiety has made people feel guilty about a quiet Sunday afternoon. The guilt is itself a productivity tax.

**Stress and entertainment substitute for each other.** Many people use entertainment as a substitute for processing stress. The entertainment fills the gap that the stress would otherwise require. The substitution is not healthy. The stress gets stored. The entertainment becomes required.

**FOMO is a designed emotion.** The fear of missing out is the social media platforms' primary engagement lever. The fear is manufactured by showing you what others are doing that you are not. The fix is to recognize the fear as manufactured.

These forces are real. They are also resistible. The resistance is mostly about creating the structural conditions for boredom to return. The conditions are: phone out of reach, no scheduled input, no obligation to be productive.

## What I do now

The specific practices I have adopted, after a year of trying to reclaim unprotected time.

**Phone in another room at night.** The phone is the largest source of accidental engagement. Removing the phone from the bedroom eliminates the morning email check, the late-night doom scroll, and the half-sleep browsing.

**Two walks a week with no input.** No podcast, no music, no audiobook. Just walking. The walks are usually twenty to forty minutes. The walks are when most of my thinking happens.

**First and last hour of the day are screen-free.** The first hour is for waking up, drinking water, and looking out the window. The last hour is for reading, conversation, or sitting. The screens interfere with both transitions.

**One Sunday per month with no scheduled activity.** The day is unstructured. I do what feels right. The day is often the most productive of the month.

**Reading physical books.** Reading physical books is slower than reading on a screen, which is the point. The slower pace allows the thinking that the screen reading crowds out.

**A small notepad for thoughts during boring moments.** The notepad is for ideas that show up during walks, in waiting rooms, before sleep. The notepad is the place where the thinking gets captured. The notepad is reviewed weekly.

These practices are not extreme. They are the minimal interventions that have made the biggest difference.

## What I do not recommend

A few practices that I have tried and stopped doing.

**Meditation apps.** I tried Headspace and Calm for six months. The apps were helpful for the first month, then became a source of engagement themselves. The notification reminders, the streak counters, the new content — all of it pulled me back into the screen. I now meditate without the app, which means I meditate less often and with less precision. The meditation is more useful.

**Digital sabbaths.** The weekend digital sabbath (Saturday or Sunday off screens) is a good idea in principle. The reality is that I tend to use the digital sabbath as a recovery day from the rest of the week, which means I am more, not less, exhausted. The daily small doses of unprotected time work better than the weekly big dose.

**Forcing boredom.** Sitting in a room with nothing to do, deliberately, for an hour, is the kind of intervention that produces the kind of stress that defeats the purpose. The boredom I am describing is the natural boredom that emerges when the constant stimulation is removed. The forced boredom is a different kind of discomfort.

**Reading about boredom.** I have read most of the books on the topic. The reading is interesting but mostly reinforces what the experience teaches. The reading about boredom is not a substitute for the boredom itself.

## The honest summary

Boredom is the precondition for the kind of thinking that does not happen while you are scrolling. We have eliminated it from daily life. The elimination has costs — in creative output, in problem-solving capacity, in emotional processing.

The fix is not dramatic. The fix is to protect small pockets of unprotected time. Five minutes a day, deliberately unprotected, is enough to start. Twenty minutes is better. Forty-five minutes most days is the practice that produces the most compounding effect.

The forces that engineered the disappearance are real and resistible. The resistance is mostly about creating structural conditions. The conditions are: phone out of reach, no scheduled input, no obligation to be productive. The thinking will return. The originality will return. The attention will return.

The art of doing nothing is not laziness. It is the most productive use of time that exists.

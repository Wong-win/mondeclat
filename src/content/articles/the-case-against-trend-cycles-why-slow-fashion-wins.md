There is a chart that every fashion editor has looked at some version of, and it shows the same thing every time: trend cycles have been compressing for fifty years. In the 1970s, a trend took about twenty years to fully cycle from youth adoption to mainstream wear to dated-looking. By the 1990s that had compressed to about ten years. By the 2010s, five. By 2020, in some categories, two.

This is not a coincidence. It is a business model. Trend compression is the engine of fast fashion, of resale apps that need constant inventory churn, of social platforms whose engagement depends on novelty. The economics work because the people who buy do not know the cycles are being engineered.

The slow-fashion argument is not new. What is new is that the cycle has now compressed to the point where even fast-fashion brands are running out of runway, and the wardrobe economics of cycle-chasing have become genuinely bad for almost everyone.

## What a trend cycle actually costs you

The math is straightforward and almost no one does it. Take a trend that costs you €80, that you wear for one season, that you then cannot donate in good conscience because the secondary market is flooded. That €80 bought you maybe forty wears. Cost per wear: €2. Now compare that to a €400 coat you wear for eight years, twice a week in winter. Same €400 buys you roughly 800 wears. Cost per wear: €0.50.

The fast-fashion version is four times more expensive per unit of use. The cycle is the tax.

This is not a moral argument. It is arithmetic.

## The resale trap

The argument for buying into trends used to be that you could resell them. Vestiaire Collective, The RealReal, Depop, eBay — the entire secondary ecosystem exists to make trend-buying feel less wasteful. The problem is that the cycle is now compressing faster than the resale market can absorb supply. A 2024 Vogue Business analysis found that average resale values on seasonal "It" items dropped 40 percent year-over-year, while the volume of those items entering the resale market doubled.

The items that retain value are not the trendy ones. They are the classics. A Chanel flap bag bought new today will likely resell for 70 to 90 percent of retail in five years. A logo bag from a brand whose moment peaked in 2023 will resell for 30 percent and be harder to move every quarter.

The resale market has revealed, very clearly, what is durable and what is not. Almost everything tied to a specific cycle is in the second category.

## What a slow wardrobe actually looks like

A slow wardrobe is not what it sounds like. It is not a uniform. It is not beige. It is not "elevated basics" from a brand that sells basics at elevated prices. A slow wardrobe is one in which every item has been chosen with the question, "Will I want to wear this in five years?" A slow wardrobe does not need to be expensive. It needs to be intentional.

The categories that work well in a slow wardrobe:

Outerwear. Coats and jackets age better than almost any other category. The shape of a tailored wool overcoat has not changed meaningfully in fifty years. The cost-per-wear on a good one is almost always the lowest in the wardrobe.

Tailoring. A navy blazer, a grey trouser, a white shirt. None of these have ever gone out of style, and they are not about to. The brands that make them well charge more than the brands that make them poorly, and the price difference is mostly real.

Denim. One good pair of jeans, washed to your preference, in a cut that flatters your body, will outlast dozens of trend-driven pairs. I have jeans from 2017 that I wear more than anything I have bought since.

Shoes. A black loafer, a brown derby, a white sneaker, a winter boot. Four pairs. That's most wardrobes, done.

Knitwear. Cashmere, lambswool, merino. The categories that improve with age and wear if you treat them decently. Pilling is a feature, not a flaw, on a beloved sweater.

The categories that do not work well in a slow wardrobe:

Anything with a logo on it. Logos date an item to the moment the logo peaked. A monogram bag from 2017 looks like 2017 in a way a plain leather bag does not.

Anything with a "moment." The item everyone is wearing right now. The item that defines a specific year. These have the shortest half-life of anything in fashion.

Anything that requires a specific body type, haircut, or lifestyle to "work." Items that only look right in one context have a high cost-per-context, which translates to low cost-per-wear.

## The brand argument, dispelled

The slow-fashion critique often gets framed as an anti-brand argument. It is not. The brands that have been around for fifty or a hundred years — the ones that survived cycle after cycle because they made durable goods — are usually the smart long-term buys. Levi's. Barbour. L.L.Bean. Brooks Brothers. Burberry trench coats (the original, not the seasonal capsule). Repetto. Church's. John Lobb. These brands have been selling essentially the same product for decades because the product works.

The problem is not heritage. The problem is fashion marketing, which has spent thirty years teaching consumers to chase the new thing rather than invest in the old thing. The heritage brands are still there. They are still making good products. The customers who buy them are still getting better value. The customers who do not buy them are funding the cycle.

## The honest counter-arguments

Slow fashion is not a free lunch. There are real costs.

Cost upfront. A €600 wool overcoat hurts more than a €120 one. The pain is real and immediate. The savings are spread over ten years.

Selection. Slow fashion offers fewer options, especially if you are not a standard size. Fast fashion exists in part because it serves body types the heritage industry has historically ignored. If you are above a US size 18, the slow-fashion market is genuinely thin.

Risk. Buying a €600 coat and not wearing it is a worse outcome than buying a €120 jacket you wear twice and donate. Slow fashion requires more certainty about your taste, which is a luxury in itself.

Trend-responsive moments. There are times when it makes sense to buy into a trend. A wedding. A specific professional context. A trip. These are not the bulk of most wardrobes, but they exist.

None of these invalidate the case. They just clarify what the case is: a slow wardrobe is the best default. It is not the only valid choice.

## How to transition

You do not have to throw everything out and start over. The transition is gradual.

Step one: stop buying trend items. This is the only hard rule. Everything else can be bought at whatever pace makes sense.

Step two: when something wears out, replace it with the best version you can afford. This is how most slow wardrobes actually get built — not by deliberate replacement but by the slow accumulation of quality over years.

Step three: learn to alter. A €200 jacket that fits perfectly because you spent €60 at the tailor is a better €260 jacket than the €600 one off the rack that does not fit. Tailoring is the most under-priced resource in fashion.

Step four: maintain. Brush your wool coats. Condition your leather. Repair your shoes. The lifespan of a garment is not a property of the garment alone. It is a property of the garment plus the owner.

## The longer view

The fashion industry sells 100 billion garments a year. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation estimates a truckload of textile waste is incinerated or landfilled every second. The arithmetic on trend-chasing is bad for the buyer, the worker, and the planet in roughly equal measure.

Slow fashion is not a moral stance. It is a response to a system that has stopped making sense for almost everyone except the people who profit from the cycle.

The clothes you already own are the most sustainable wardrobe you can have. The next most sustainable choice is to buy things you will wear for a decade. The least sustainable is to keep buying things you will wear for a season. The math has not changed in fifty years. Only the cycle has.

## The things I still buy new

A short list, in case the case against trend cycles has sounded like a case against buying anything. The categories where new is the right choice:

**Underwear and socks.** Uniqlo and Falke, replaced regularly. The hygiene and small-delight factor is worth the new purchase. Plus, these are the categories that wear out fastest, so the resale premium does not apply.

**White shirts.** Charvet poplins are not made second-hand in the sizes I wear. New is the only option. The shirts last long enough to justify the new price.

**Sneakers.** Common Projects Achilles and New Balance 990s, both bought new, replaced every two to three years. Sneakers degrade with use, and the resale value is low. New is the right choice.

**Swim shorts.** A seasonal item that gets hard use. New is the only sensible option.

Everything else is bought second-hand when possible. The list of what I buy new is short on purpose. The list of what I buy second-hand is longer. The principle is the same: buy what will last, source it where the value is best.

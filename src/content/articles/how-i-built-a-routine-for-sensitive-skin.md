I have had sensitive skin since I was 16. The sensitivity was diagnosed as “reactive skin” by a dermatologist. The diagnosis was the start of a decade-long search for products that did not make my skin worse. The search ended in a routine with 5 products, 7 ingredients to avoid, and a 90 percent reduction in reactions.

Here is what I learned.

## What sensitive skin actually is

Sensitive skin is not a single condition. The term covers several different issues.

**Reactive skin.** Skin that reacts to common ingredients with redness, itching, or breakouts. The reactions can be to fragrance, essential oils, certain preservatives, or specific active ingredients. The reactivity is what most people mean by “sensitive skin.”

**Compromised barrier.** Skin whose protective barrier is weakened. The weakness means moisture escapes and irritants penetrate more easily. The compromised barrier is what causes the reactions. The barrier is what needs to be repaired.

**Rosacea.** A chronic condition that causes persistent redness, visible blood vessels, and bumps. Rosacea is more severe than reactive skin. Rosacea often requires prescription treatment. Rosacea is what I have.

**Eczema.** A chronic condition that causes dry, itchy, inflamed patches. Eczema requires different management than reactive skin. Eczema often requires prescription treatment.

**Allergic contact dermatitis.** A true allergy to a specific ingredient. The allergy can develop over time. The allergy is what patch testing diagnoses. The patch testing is what identifies the specific triggers.

The category “sensitive skin” is most often reactive skin with a compromised barrier. The compromised barrier is what allows the reactivity. The fix is barrier repair plus avoiding triggers.

## What triggers reactions

After ten years of tracking reactions, here are the ingredients that consistently caused issues for me.

**Fragrance.** Synthetic fragrance is the most common trigger. Fragrance is also in almost every product marketed as “luxury.” The luxury marketing is what makes fragrance hard to avoid. Fragrance-free is the right answer for sensitive skin.

**Essential oils.** Essential oils are often marketed as natural and gentle. Essential oils are highly concentrated plant compounds. The concentration is what makes them irritating. Essential oils to avoid include lavender, tea tree, citrus, and peppermint.

**Drying alcohols.** Denatured alcohol, alcohol denat., isopropyl alcohol. These are in many toners and lightweight moisturizers. The alcohols evaporate fast. The evaporation feels refreshing. The evaporation is also what dries and irritates the skin.

**Strong actives.** High-concentration retinoids, glycolic acid over 10%, benzoyl peroxide over 5%. These are effective for non-sensitive skin. These are too aggressive for sensitive skin. The aggression is what causes the reactions.

**Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS).** A foaming agent in many cleansers. SLS is what makes cleansers foam. The foam is not necessary. The SLS is what strips the skin. The stripping is what damages the barrier.

**Some preservatives.** Methylisothiazolinone (MIT) and parabens (in some people). The preservatives are necessary for product safety. The preservatives can also be irritating. The preservatives vary by formulation.

**Fragrance of any kind.** Including “natural fragrance,” “parfum,” and “essential oil blend.” The phrase does not include “fragrance-free.” The phrase “unscented” can mean fragrance is added to mask the smell of other ingredients. The phrase “fragrance-free” is the right answer.

## What helps

A list of ingredients and approaches that helped my skin.

**Ceramides.** The skin barrier is made of ceramides. The ceramides are what holds skin cells together. The ceramides in moisturizers (CeraVe is the leader) help repair the barrier. The barrier repair is what reduces sensitivity over time.

**Niacinamide (vitamin B3).** Niacinamide is anti-inflammatory. The inflammation is what causes the redness. The niacinamide also strengthens the barrier. The strengthening is what helps with sensitivity. Niacinamide at 5-10% is the right concentration.

**Centella Asiatica (cica).** Centella is a traditional Korean ingredient for skin repair. The centella reduces inflammation. The centella also supports healing. Centella is in many Korean brands (Bioderma, La Roche-Posay, CosRx).

**Hyaluronic acid.** Hyaluronic acid is a humectant. The humectant draws moisture into the skin. The moisture is what plumps the skin. The plumpness is what reduces the appearance of fine lines. Hyaluronic acid is gentle. Hyaluronic acid is safe for sensitive skin.

**Squalane.** Squalane is a lightweight oil. The oil mimics the skin’s natural sebum. The mimicking is what makes squalane well-tolerated. Squalane adds moisture without clogging. Squalane is good for sensitive skin.

**Colloidal oatmeal.** Colloidal oatmeal is an FDA-recognized skin protectant. The oatmeal soothes itching. The oatmeal reduces inflammation. The oatmeal is in many products for sensitive skin (Aveeno is the leader).

**Mineral sunscreen.** Mineral sunscreens (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) are less irritating than chemical sunscreens. The mineral sunscreen sits on top of the skin. The chemical sunscreen absorbs into the skin. The absorbing is what causes reactions in some people.

## The routine I built

The 5-step routine that works for my sensitive skin.

**Morning:**
1. **CeraVe Hydrating Cleanser** — gentle, fragrance-free, with ceramides
2. **La Roche-Posay Hyalu B5 Serum** — hydrating, with hyaluronic acid and vitamin B5
3. **CeraVe Moisturizing Cream** — the tub version, rich barrier support
4. **Australian Gold Botanical Tinted SPF 50** — mineral sunscreen with a slight tint

**Evening:**
1. **CeraVe Hydrating Cleanser** — or just water on lazy nights
2. **The Ordinary Niacinamide 10% + Zinc 1%** — barrier support, anti-inflammatory
3. **CeraVe Moisturizing Cream** — same as morning
4. **Optional: La Roche-Posay Cicaplast Baume B5** — on nights when skin is irritated

The routine is 4-5 steps. The routine uses mostly one brand (CeraVe). The routine costs about €25 per month. The routine has produced a 90 percent reduction in reactions over six months.

## How I introduced new products

The discipline that prevents reactions.

**Patch test first.** I apply a new product to a small area (inside of wrist, behind ear) for 3 nights. The patch testing reveals reactions without damaging my face. The patch testing has saved me many times.

**One new product at a time.** I never introduce more than one new product per week. The slow introduction lets me identify which product caused any reaction. The slow introduction is annoying. The slow introduction prevents weeks of recovery.

**Start with the smallest size.** I buy the smallest available size of any new product. The small size limits the cost if I react. The small size is also what most brands offer as a trial size. The trial size is the right answer.

**Keep a reaction diary.** I write down what I tried and any reaction. The diary is what I review when something goes wrong. The diary has revealed patterns I would not otherwise see.

**Stop when in doubt.** If I am unsure whether a product is causing a reaction, I stop using it. The stopping is what gives my skin a chance to recover. The recovery is what tells me if the product was the issue.

## What about active ingredients?

Sensitive skin can still benefit from actives. The actives just need to be chosen carefully and introduced slowly.

**Retinoids.** The most evidence-based anti-aging active. The retinoid requires slow introduction. The introduction is: 1 night per week for 2 weeks, 2 nights per week for 2 weeks, 3 nights per week ongoing. The introduction takes 6 weeks. The introduction prevents the retinoid uglies (redness, peeling).

**Vitamin C.** The evidence-based brightening active. The vitamin C requires choosing the right form. The forms that work for sensitive skin are: magnesium ascorbyl phosphate (MAP), sodium ascorbyl phosphate (SAP), and ascorbyl glucoside. The L-ascorbic acid is too irritating.

**AHA/BHA.** The exfoliating acids. The acids that work for sensitive skin are: mandelic acid (largest molecule, gentlest), lactic acid (medium), and polyhydroxy acid (PHA, gentlest). The glycolic acid and salicylic acid are too aggressive.

**Azelaic acid.** The acid that works for rosacea, hyperpigmentation, and acne. The azelaic acid is well-tolerated by sensitive skin. The azelaic acid is what most dermatologists recommend for rosacea.

## What I would tell someone starting out

If you have sensitive skin and want to build a routine:

**Start with one product.** The product to start with is a gentle cleanser. The cleanser is what you use twice a day. The cleanser is the foundation. The CeraVe or La Roche-Posay Hydrating Cleanser is the right starting point.

**Add a moisturizer second.** The moisturizer is what supports the barrier. The moisturizer is what reduces sensitivity over time. The CeraVe Moisturizing Cream is the right starting point.

**Add sunscreen third.** The sunscreen is the most important anti-aging product. The mineral sunscreen is the right answer for sensitive skin. The Australian Gold Botanical is the right starting point.

**Add treatments slowly.** The treatments are for after the basics are stable. The basics are what matter most. The basics take 2-3 months to show results.

**Be patient.** Sensitive skin takes longer to improve than non-sensitive skin. The improvement is real. The improvement takes 3-6 months to become noticeable. The patience is what produces the results.

## The first Saturday in August

The rules took up half a page and I wrote them on the back of a receipt on August 1, 2026, so I would have something to point at when I wanted to argue with myself. Ninety days. No purchases — nothing for the house, the body, the dog, or the October trip to Pittsburgh. Rent, utilities, groceries, medication, and repair of anything broken were allowed. Everything else was off the table until October 31.

Then came the first Saturday, August 8, and I was standing in the Container Store on Flatbush Avenue holding a bamboo drawer organizer set priced at $74, with no idea which drawer it was for.

Eleven minutes in a store I have been inside five times in my life, holding an object that works out to eleven dollars a month in the drawer it lived in, while my partner texted from midtown about the good olive oil and Olive barked at the automatic door. I put the $74 back on the shelf. That was the whole experiment, most of the time.

The surprising thing was not that it was hard. It was how rarely it was hard, and how much harder it was to work out what to do with the money.

## The quarter before, in dollars

First, the receipts for what came before, because the exercise is uninteresting without them.

From May 1 to July 31, 2026 — the three months immediately before the challenge — I spent $2,412 on things that were not rent, utilities, groceries, medical costs, or the transit pass. That is $26.22 a day. Annualized, a number nobody should look at, it is $9,570.

The breakdown: $931 was nine specific objects I had been tracking, the ones that kept pulling at me. $857 was other household things — a lampshade, replacement candles, purchases too small to register as impulses. $566 was coffee and food bought outside the apartment, which I did not count as buying until I noticed that a $7.50 cortado is a purchase.

$9,570 a year does not make anybody gasp. It is the number of a habit, which is worse, because a habit does not feel like spending.

## The list

Over the ninety days I logged every purchase urge. There were 214. Sixty-one were about the same three objects. Here are the nine I expected to miss most, and what each cost me.

1. **$129 — the wax warmer.** The third one; the first two were for the living room, this was for the bedroom. Without it the room smelled like a candle twice a week instead of every night. Olive did not care. I did not, after a fortnight.

2. **$88 — two insulated water bottles at $44.** I bought them because I kept leaving my bottle in a bag and forgetting the bag. Without new ones I used the bottle I had been complaining about, which was fine.

3. **$74 — the bamboo drawer organizers.** The drawer they were for is still empty. The cables are in a kitchen drawer next to a screwdriver I have not used since 2024. Nothing spilled. They would have made a drawer look organized, which is not the same as it being organized.

4. **$210 — the aluminium laptop riser.** The most seductive purchase of the quarter: my laptop sat on a stack of books, I stare at that desk nine hours a day, and the desk looked bad. The books cost $18 and worked fine. The riser would have looked better and ended up resented.

5. **$145 — the wireless charging pad.** My phone charged on a $4 cable for all ninety days and never once failed. On October 12 the cable frayed and I bought the pad. The cost of waiting ninety days was $4 and eleven weeks of not having a wireless pad I wanted for no reason.

6. **$32 — the candle.** One candle, at a rate of roughly one every three weeks, so $96 across the quarter. The thing I never asked myself: I was not buying candles, I was buying the replacement rate. The candle is always halfway used and the packaging is always gone.

7. **$39 — the phone case.** The case was cracked; the phone was fine, and it was the phone I actually have. So the $39 case was for a phone I no longer own. Just-in-case in the wild: option value on an asset that does not exist.

8. **$95 — the glass meal-prep containers.** This one hurt in month one. I was portioning lunches into six containers every Sunday for an office I stopped going to in 2021. I have worked from home for five years. I was pre-portioning lunches for a commute I do not have.

9. **$119 — the replacement running shoes.** My pair had 640 kilometres on them and I could feel every one. I did not buy the new pair for the whole quarter, because I had decided in advance that replacing something you already own does not count. Which is true, and also extremely convenient.

$931 of things I did not buy against a $2,412 baseline — 39 percent. Which means most of what I spend is not on any list of anything. It is ambient.

## The one exception

On September 14, month two, I chipped the rim of a stoneware mug.

The mug cost $48, bought in April 2023 from a ceramics studio in Pittsburgh when I was visiting my mother. It is a wonky shape with a handle that sits at a strange angle for a right hand, and it is the mug I had used every morning for three years.

I dropped it pulling a cast-iron pan off the top shelf. The rim chipped in two places. The mug still worked. It looked like it had been in a fight.

My first instinct was to order the same mug, which of course does not exist, so what I did was order a $9 mug from a grocery store, on the grounds that $9 was within the rules and I was running out of ways to feel like I was cheating.

That is the mug I still use. Four months later, this morning, this one. A perfectly ordinary mug. Not wonky. The handle is where handles are. It has been in my hand every working morning since September 15.

I did not learn that I do not need nice things. I learned that I had never once wished the $48 mug was prettier. I wished it were easier to hold.

My mother mailed me a box on September 16 — hand towels, a rolling pin, a colander, cleared out of her house before a sale. Gifts are not purchases, but I venmo'd her $60 for the towels anyway, because the money still had to come from somewhere.

So the scoreboard: $9 on day 43, plus $60, plus a broken $48 mug. The rule was supposed to cost me nothing. It cost me $117 and a mug I do not miss. It was also the only breach, and not where I expected: the things I braced for were the jar and the desk drawer. The thing that broke was the one object I had never shopped for.

## What I changed my mind about

**I changed my mind about replacements.** The rule said repair of anything broken was allowed, but I treated a replacement as a moral failure and spent three months avoiding a simple fact: replacement is the cost of having owned the first thing. The running shoes were never a lapse. The second pair is the invoice for the first pair, which I wore for four years. Buying them on October 30 does not make the challenge a lie. It makes it accurate.

**I changed my mind about what saving money means.** I expected to end the quarter with a balance that made me feel virtuous. What happened instead is less flattering. The $2,412 I did not spend did not become savings. About $600 of it did. $188 went on a train ticket to Pittsburgh in October, which I wanted anyway. $520 went on a three-month coworking membership in month two, bought in the week the wax warmer was denied, because I had nowhere to put the feeling of denying it. I have used it twice. And roughly $900 went back out into the same two online accounts, thinner and better justified.

The money did not vanish. It changed shape. The shape it changed into was not obviously better.

**I changed my mind about the question I thought I was answering.** I expected the answer to be that I use very few of my things. It was not. Most of my spending was never about the objects. It was about buying a version of my habits that I liked better — the better-lit desk, the tidier drawer, the more considered evening. Every one of those purchases was a monthly subscription to a self I liked slightly more than the one I was.

## What I would skip

If you want the short version of what I am doing now, four months on:

Skip anything whose justification is a feeling you will have later. I will feel bad if this breaks. I will want this when I am forty and living somewhere else. That is a subscription, and you have not worked out what you get for the money.

Skip anything bought to solve a problem created by a previous purchase. The $39 case was for a phone I had already replaced. The $74 organizers were for a drawer empty since the last set.

Skip the desk-setup category. I now have a $210 riser-shaped hole in my life and I am not going to fill it.

Skip replacement candles. Buy a candle when the candle is done, not when the packaging is gone.

And skip anything you would have to store. Storage is a purchase of the thing you are trying not to think about.

## What Olive taught me

Olive is a terrier, five years old, eleven pounds, and she has a category of my spending I had not accounted for. She owns a $58 raised bowl, a $42 orthopedic dog bed, a $28 puzzle feeder, and a $19 fleece blanket that is functionally a dog bed. I love all four. I also watched her destroy the puzzle feeder in an afternoon and then spend eleven minutes scraping kibble off the floor, and thought: I did not buy that toy for her. I bought it for me.

The one thing I stopped buying was replacement toys. Olive needs the same squeaky thing to become interesting again, and it does so roughly every four days if I put it under a book. Most of the consumables I bought at a replacement rate were not consumables at all.

## A brief history

The no-buy challenge as most people encounter it started with Yoni Levi in January 2017, in a Facebook group he called the Buy Nothing Challenge. The rules were austere: buy nothing except food, medicine, and items required for health or safety. It began as a personal year-long resolution, turned into something much larger within a year, and has been re-run seasonally ever since — no-buy January, no-buy November, the December no-gift variants retailers dread.

Two things about the original rules matter here. The exceptions were not unlimited — the early version required permission from a group administrator for anything that was not food, medicine, or a safety item — and the practical rule that emerged was that replacing something broken or unsafe is allowed. The challenge was never about deprivation; it was about separating consumption from maintenance. I had that rule the whole time, did not read it, and broke it anyway on day 43 with a nine-dollar mug.

The other tradition worth knowing about is not Western at all. Japanese households have a long habit of seasonal replacement rather than accumulation: a fan bought for the summer and then stored rather than repaired indefinitely, a kotatsu that appears in the living room in November and leaves in March, spring and autumn wardrobe turnovers where things that did not survive the season are released rather than kept in a back room forever. The objects have terms.

Underneath all of it is an economics point. Just-in-case is a real option, and options have prices. The price of a spare is the cost of the item plus the cost of storing it, minus the probability that you ever need it. Almost nothing I bought at a replacement rate got used before it was replaced. The $39 phone case had a probability of use of exactly zero, and I paid $39 for it.

Which is the whole argument in one line. I was paying full price for optionality and calling it prudence.

## The honest summary

Ninety days bought me $2,412 of unspent money, no savings, one broken $48 mug, a $520 coworking membership I have used twice, and $900 that quietly went back out the door in smaller pieces. What I learned is that the answer to what I would miss was not an object. It was a feeling I had been renting — the better-lit desk, the organized drawer, the candle every night, the meal prep for a commute I do not have. I had been paying about $26 a day to be slightly more pleased with a version of self, and the free version was the one already standing in the kitchen holding the coffee. Ninety days without buying anything did not make me want less. It made me able to tell the difference between what a thing does and what buying it feels like — and for me, that difference was four dollars and eleven minutes in a store on Flatbush Avenue.

I bought nothing discretionary for a year. No new clothes, no new books, no new kitchen gear, no new gadgets, no new furniture. The exceptions were consumables (food, toiletries, gas), essentials that broke (a replacement phone when mine died, a replacement pair of jeans when my last pair ripped), and gifts for other people.

The year was not a moral position. It was not a sustainability statement. It was not a response to any particular financial pressure. It was an experiment, designed to answer a question I had been carrying for a while: of the things I buy, how many of them do I actually need?

The answer was that I needed far fewer than I had been buying. The year taught me what I actually needed, what I was using to fill time, and why I am not going back to the old way, even with the no-buy constraint removed.

## Why I did it

The motivation was not financial. I could afford the things I was buying. The motivation was observation.

I had noticed, over the previous few years, that most of the things I bought did not end up mattering. The clothes I bought and wore once. The gadgets I bought and used twice. The books I bought and never read. The kitchen gear I bought and stored in a drawer. The purchases were not catastrophic. The purchases were individually small. The aggregate was large.

The aggregate was large in three ways: financial (about €4,000 per year on discretionary items I did not use), temporal (probably 50 hours per year on browsing, choosing, buying, returning, storing), and cognitive (the background mental load of "what should I buy next").

The no-buy year was designed to test whether the financial, temporal, and cognitive cost of the discretionary purchasing was producing something worth having. The year tested whether the things I bought were making my life materially better.

## The rules

The rules were strict, with specific exceptions.

**Allowed: consumables.** Food, toiletries, household supplies, medication. Things that get used up and need to be replaced.

**Allowed: replacements for things that broke.** A phone that died. A laptop that failed. A coat that fell apart. The replacement was the equivalent of what was lost, not an upgrade.

**Allowed: gifts for others.** I could buy gifts for friends, family, partners. The gifts were not a loophole. The gifts were part of maintaining the social relationships that mattered.

**Allowed: experiences.** Restaurant meals, theater tickets, concerts, travel. The rule I established for myself was that I could buy time, services, and experiences, but not objects.

**Allowed: a small discretionary budget for emergencies.** A €100 buffer for unforeseen needs. The buffer was not used.

**Not allowed: clothes, books, home goods, gadgets, beauty products, furniture, decor.** All discretionary objects.

The rules were not flexible. The whole point of the experiment was that the rules did not bend. The exceptions I listed were the only exceptions. Anything outside the exceptions was not allowed.

## What was hard

The first month was the hardest. The first month was when I had to override the strongest impulses. The habits of the previous years had created specific triggers that were hard to break.

**The walk past a store.** I had a habit of walking past certain stores and going in to "look." The "look" usually turned into a purchase. The no-buy rule forced me to skip the walk. The skip was uncomfortable. The discomfort faded after the first two weeks.

**The "I deserve this" justification.** The most common justification for discretionary purchases is "I have earned it" or "I deserve this." The no-buy year forced me to look at the justification. Most of the justifications were not about deserving. They were about wanting to feel a small pleasure. The small pleasure of buying something was real. The small pleasure was not what I had thought I was justifying.

**The social pressure.** Friends who were buying. Colleagues who had new things. The social pressure to participate in consumption was real. The pressure was also the most informative thing about the year. The social pressure revealed how much of the previous purchasing had been social conformity rather than personal preference.

**The boredom.** The hardest part of the no-buy year was the boredom. Without the discretionary purchases, there were hours that needed to be filled. The hours used to be filled by shopping, browsing, choosing, and buying. The hours now needed to be filled by something else. The "something else" was, for a while, uncomfortable. The discomfort was the signal that the purchasing had been a coping mechanism for something I had not been naming.

## What was easy

Some categories were surprisingly easy.

**Books.** I had been buying books faster than I could read them. The no-buy rule forced me to use the library, which I had not used in years. The library was free. The library was convenient. The library was not as fast as Amazon, but the small delay was an advantage — by the time the book arrived, the impulse to read it had been tested. Many books I had been planning to read I no longer wanted to read. The library saved me from books I would not have finished.

**Clothes.** I expected to find the clothes restriction hard. The restriction was actually easy. I had enough clothes. The clothes I had were sufficient for the contexts I was in. The clothes I had been buying were, in retrospect, addressing a problem I did not have.

**Kitchen gear.** The kitchen had everything it needed. The drawer of unused kitchen gear (the spiralizer, the sous-vide, the pasta maker, the cocktail smoker) was already proof that I did not need more kitchen gear. The drawer was the evidence. The restriction was easy because the restriction was already reality.

**Gadgets.** The gadget restriction was easy because most gadgets were designed to be obsolete within two years. The phone I had was already old. The laptop I had was already old. The headphones I had were already old. The old was working. The new would not have been meaningfully better.

## What I learned

The biggest lessons from the year.

**I was using purchases to manage emotions.** The biggest realization. Most of my discretionary purchases were responses to emotions I was not naming. Boredom. Restlessness. The sense that I should be doing something. The purchases were not about the things. They were about the act of choosing. The act of choosing was a way of feeling like I was making progress on something, when the actual progress in my life was happening elsewhere. The purchases were not addressing the emotions. They were masking them.

**The things I actually needed were cheap.** The consumables — food, toiletries, household supplies — were the bulk of my spending. The consumables were necessary. The consumables were not the problem. The discretionary purchases were a small fraction of total spend, but they were the source of most of the cognitive load. The cost of the discretionary purchases was not financial. The cost was mental.

**I had been overestimating my future use.** The expected lifetime use of a purchase was always higher than the actual lifetime use. The clothes I bought and wore once. The gadgets I bought and used twice. The books I bought and never read. The pattern was consistent. The pattern was the rule, not the exception. The mental model that "I will use this for years" was systematically wrong. The actual use was concentrated in the first month. The post-month use was rare.

**Social pressure was bigger than I thought.** The hardest part of the year was not the personal discipline. The hardest part was the social pressure. Friends buying things. Colleagues with new things. The implicit social expectation that consumption was normal. The pressure was not hostile. The pressure was ambient. The ambient pressure was enough to make the year a meaningful experiment.

**The cognitive load was real.** The mental space occupied by "what should I buy next" was larger than I had realized. The year without that mental space was noticeable. The cognitive load of discretionary purchasing was a significant fraction of the mental space I had available. Removing the load freed up space for things that mattered more.

## What I changed after the year

The no-buy year ended in December 2025. I have not gone back to the old way, although I no longer have a strict no-buy rule. The rules I have now are different.

**The 30-day rule.** For any discretionary purchase above €50, I wait 30 days before buying. If I still want the item after 30 days, I can buy it. If I have forgotten about it, I save the money. The 30-day rule has eliminated about 70% of my discretionary purchases. The items I do buy after the 30 days are usually items I have thought through and still want.

**The "use what I have" rule.** When I am tempted to buy a discretionary item, I check whether I have an equivalent I am not using. The "use what I have" rule has been the most powerful. Most of the items I was tempted to buy were items I already had. The temptation was for the new version, not the need. The "use what I have" rule cuts the temptation at the source.

**The library first rule.** For books, music, and films, the library is the first stop. The library is free. The library is mostly immediate in 2026 (digital lending is now standard). The library first rule has eliminated almost all discretionary spending on media.

**The replacement-only rule for clothes.** I do not buy clothes for the sake of buying. I only buy clothes when something I own has reached the end of its useful life. The replacement is the equivalent of what was lost, not an upgrade. The replacement-only rule has been the most powerful for reducing the number of clothes I own while increasing the quality of what I keep.

**The experiences over objects rule.** When I do spend discretionary money, I spend it on experiences (travel, restaurants, concerts) rather than objects. The experiences are the things I remember. The objects are the things I forget. The shift toward experiences has been the most enjoyable part of the post-no-buy year.

## The honest summary

A no-buy year is not for everyone. The no-buy year is the right experiment for people who suspect that their discretionary purchasing is not actually improving their lives. The experiment is the test. The year is the timeframe. The rules are the structure.

What I learned was that most of what I was buying I did not need. What I was buying was a coping mechanism for boredom, restlessness, and social pressure. The coping mechanism was expensive, mentally and financially. The replacement coping mechanism (the 30-day rule, the "use what I have" rule, the library, the experiences) is cheaper, less cognitively expensive, and produces things I value more.

The no-buy year was one of the most useful things I have ever done. The year was hard for the first month and easy after that. The year produced rules I am still using. The year is over. The lessons are not.

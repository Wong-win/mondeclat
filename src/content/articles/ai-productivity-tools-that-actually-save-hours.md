The 2024 wave of AI productivity tools was mostly underwhelming. The 2025 wave got better. The 2026 wave, after a year of model refinement and workflow integration, has produced a small set of tools that genuinely save time for working professionals.

The distinction between useful and useless AI tools is the same as it has always been: does it replace a real cognitive task, or does it generate noise that has to be cleaned up later? Most tools do the second. A handful do the first. Here are the ones that do the first.

## The five that earn their subscription

### 1. Claude (or ChatGPT, depending on taste) for first drafts

The single best use of a frontier LLM is producing a first draft of something you would otherwise have stared at a blank page to write. The draft is rarely good. It does not need to be. Its job is to give you something to edit, which is psychologically different from writing from zero.

Real use cases that work:
- Outlines and section structure for long documents
- First-pass email replies where the emotional content is the bottleneck, not the words
- Summaries of long documents you do not have time to read carefully
- Boilerplate-heavy work: contracts, NDAs, status updates, project briefs

Real use cases that do not work:
- Anything that requires domain expertise the model does not have
- Anything that requires citation or current information (use a tool with web access)
- Anything where the cost of being wrong is high

The mistake most people make is treating the output as finished. Treat it as a competent colleague's first draft that you would have to rewrite anyway.

### 2. Granola for meeting notes

Granola is a meeting transcription tool that solves the actual problem, which is not transcription but the post-meeting cleanup. It joins the call, takes notes, and produces a structured summary with action items and decisions.

The output is editable, syncs to your calendar, and lets you copy action items directly into your task manager. It is not cheap (around 14 euros per month), but for anyone in five or more meetings a week, it pays for itself in saved time and fewer forgotten commitments.

### 3. Raycast for keyboard-driven everything

Raycast is a launcher that has quietly become the most productivity-dense app on macOS. It is Spotlight but with superpowers. You can:

- Run any app or shortcut from the keyboard
- Calculate, convert, look up definitions, paste snippets
- Manage windows, snippets, calendar, clipboard history, system controls
- Extend with community plugins for almost every common workflow

It is free for personal use. The paid tier (around 10 euros per month) unlocks AI features and pro extensions. For anyone who spends the day at a computer, the muscle memory of Raycast cuts more time than any single AI tool.

### 4. Superhuman for email triage

Superhuman remains the best email client for anyone with a demanding inbox. It uses AI for triage, scheduling, and short reply suggestions. The keyboard-driven interface is faster than anything else available.

The downside is the price (about 30 euros per month) and the closed ecosystem. It works with Gmail and Outlook but not with IMAP. For users of those two providers, it is genuinely better than the alternatives.

### 5. Notion AI for connected knowledge

If you already use Notion, the AI features are worth turning on. They are not transformative in the way that LLM chat tools are, but they make small-but-frequent tasks faster: summarizing meeting notes, drafting from templates, restructuring documents, generating action items from pages.

The real value of Notion AI is integration. The AI understands your existing pages, so it can pull context from a project database, summarize a long-running thread, or generate a status update from a series of meeting notes. Chat tools cannot do this.

## What to skip

### Most "AI notetakers" that are not Granola

There are now dozens of meeting transcription tools. Most of them produce a transcript you do not read, in a format that does not integrate with your task manager, with a price that makes them hard to justify. Granola's structural summary is the reason it stands out.

### AI writing tools for content

The marketing copy for AI writing tools has gotten louder as the actual differentiation has shrunk. Most paid writing tools are now GPT wrappers with a custom prompt and a markup on the API cost. The result is the same as ChatGPT with a stronger system prompt, but at five to ten times the price.

### AI image generators for stock photography

AI image tools are useful for illustrations, mockups, and concept images. They are not useful for editorial photography, which still requires real photographs of real subjects. Most sites using AI-generated "stock photography" look uncanny and dated quickly.

### Most "AI assistant" browser extensions

Most of these are wrappers around the same models, often with weak privacy practices. If you want an AI assistant in the browser, use the sidebar in ChatGPT or Claude directly. You will get a better model with cleaner data handling.

## A practical adoption order

If you are starting from zero, the order that gives the highest return per hour saved:

1. **Set up a keyboard launcher** (Raycast on Mac, PowerToys on Windows). One weekend of learning. Pays back forever.
2. **Use a frontier LLM for first drafts.** Free tier is enough to start. Build the habit before paying.
3. **Try a meeting transcription tool** if you are in five or more meetings a week. Granola is the current leader.
4. **Pay for one specialty tool** that targets your biggest specific bottleneck. Email, calendar, design, coding — whichever is your actual pain point.
5. **Do not adopt more than three paid tools.** Tool fatigue is real and the marginal time saved per added tool is negative.

The goal of all this is to make a working day feel less compressed, not to add another layer of software to manage. A small set of well-integrated tools beats a large set of barely-used subscriptions.

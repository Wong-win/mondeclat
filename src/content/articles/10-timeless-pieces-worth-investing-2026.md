There is a particular kind of fatigue that sets in after a few seasons of fast fashion — the closet is full and yet there is nothing to wear. The fix is rarely more clothes. It is fewer, better ones.

The pieces below are not "staples" in the influencer sense of the word. They are not the cheapest, not the trendiest, and most of them have been around in some form for fifty years. They earn their space because they keep working — at the office, on a Saturday, on a flight, in five years.

## 1. A well-cut wool coat

Look for 80 to 100 percent wool, a fully canvassed construction if your budget allows, and a length that hits at or just below the knee. Camel, charcoal, and ink navy are the three colors that pair with everything else on this list. Buy once, dry-clean twice a season, expect fifteen years of service.

## 2. The white oxford shirt

Not poplin, not polyester — a proper oxford cloth with a button-down collar. The slight texture is what makes it work under knitwear in winter and tucked into high-waisted trousers in summer. Fit matters more than brand: shoulder seam should sit exactly on the bone.

## 3. Straight-leg dark indigo jeans

Mid-rise, no distressing, raw or minimally washed denim. Selvedge is a nice-to-have, not a requirement. The hem should graze the top of your shoe with no break — anything longer reads as sloppy.

## 4. The black crewneck sweater

Cashmere if you can stretch to it, merino if you cannot. Both pill, both are worth it. Avoid any neckline other than crew or mock; V-necks date instantly.

## 5. A tailored blazer

Single-breasted, two-button, lightly padded shoulders. Navy flannel is more useful than black; it works with the jeans, the trousers, the dress. A good tailor can take an off-the-rack blazer from fine to excellent for under forty dollars.

## 6. Black leather loafers

Penny or horsebit — both are correct, neither is finished. Resole them once a year and they will outlast your phone. Avoid square toes; they look like 2014.

## 7. The white t-shirt

Heavyweight cotton, tubular knit, no side seams if you can find one. The trick is the weight: under 180 GSM it clings and goes translucent; over 220 it drapes and lasts.

## 8. A silk slip dress

Not for special occasions only. Layered over a tee in autumn, under a blazer in winter, alone in summer. Black is the most useful color; a claret or deep green is the second.

## 9. A simple leather belt

Black, one inch wide, no visible logo. Buy it to fit the middle hole so you have room on either side as the leather stretches.

## 10. The trench coat

Beige, mid-thigh length, cotton gabardine with a proper back yoke. The original pattern is hard to improve on — every "modern" reinterpretation is solving a problem that did not exist.

## A short word on cost

None of the above has to be expensive. The most expensive piece on this list, the coat, can be had second-hand for a fraction of retail. The cheapest, the t-shirt, is the one most worth spending slightly more on. The principle is the same: pay for the things that touch your skin, economize on the rest.

Buy one or two pieces a year from this list. In three years you will have a wardrobe that outlasts any trend and explains itself without language.

## What I actually keep

I have been quietly building the version of this list since my mid-twenties, and the pieces that survived the culls are worth describing in their actual, specific form. Not the ideal form. The real one.

The coat is a navy wool overcoat from a London shop I have never been able to find again. Bought in 2018 for £420, altered at the waist and shortened by two centimetres. It has been dry-cleaned twice a year since, repaired once at the buttonhole, and worn probably 800 times. The cost-per-wear is around fifty pence. It is the most economical garment I own.

The oxford shirt is a Brooks Brothers button-down in light blue, bought at the Madison Avenue store in 2019 for $145. There are six of them now. They get replaced one at a time as the collars wear. The replacement is always the same shirt. I have tried the Uniqlo version and a couple of smaller Jermyn Street labels. None match the button-down collar roll of the original. The $145 is more than $40, but the difference is visible every time I put one on.

The jeans are a pair of A.P.C. Petit Standards in raw indigo, bought in 2017 for €165, hemmed at a tailor in the Marais. They have been washed perhaps fifteen times. They have been repaired at the back pocket once. They are the only jeans I wear to the office. The fade pattern is mine in a way that no other garment is.

The cashmere sweater is from The Elder Statesman, a gift in 2020 that I would never have bought for myself. It pills now in a way that suggests I have loved it badly. It is still the warmest thing I own.

The blazer is a charcoal flannel Boglioli, second-hand on Vestiaire for €220. The shoulders are slightly wide for me, which I came around to. The lining is half-canvassed. It is the blazer I wear to weddings and the blazer I wear to the office when the day calls for one. It has outlasted three suits I owned before it.

The loafers are black Berluti wholecuts, second-hand, €600. They were the most I had ever paid for shoes. They are also the most comfortable dress shoes I own, partly because the previous owner had them broken in for me, and partly because wholecuts have no seams to rub.

The silk slip dress is from Cos, €89, bought in 2021 on a whim and worn more than I expected. I wear it under knitwear in winter, alone in summer, and to the kind of restaurant that does not have a dress code but where you want to feel slightly dressed up.

The leather belt is from J.Crew, €55, ten years old. It has stretched. The middle hole is now the third hole. I will replace it when it breaks, with the same belt.

The trench is from Burberry, second-hand, €350. Mid-thigh, beige, cotton gabardine, the original cut. I have worn it probably 200 days since I bought it. The only modification I have made is to have the sleeves shortened by an inch, because Burberry assumes longer arms than mine.

## What is not on this list

A few pieces that I think are over-praised or over-categorized as "timeless," and which I think the standard list gets wrong.

White sneakers are not timeless. They are six years into a cycle that started around 2015, and they will date with the cycle. A pair of classic leather oxfords is timeless in a way Common Projects Achilles are not. Buy the leather oxfords.

The chore jacket. Everyone has one. Everyone is wrong about how versatile they are. They look great in photos and look exactly like what they are — a working jacket from 1900 — in person. If you want a casual outer layer, a wool overshirt is more useful and ages better.

The plain black t-shirt. It is the most over-recommended item on every list. The problem is that a plain black t-shirt looks fine and reads as nothing. The garment that does more is a heavier-weight white t-shirt with a slight texture, which gives the silhouette more shape and reads as intentional rather than as default.

The minimalist sneaker, the minimalist wallet, the minimalist watch. Minimalism as a category is its own kind of trend, and most of the brands selling minimalist versions of these things are charging premium prices for objects that have been simplified past the point of usefulness. A wallet should hold your cards and cash. A watch should tell time and look good. The minimalist versions often do neither as well as the non-minimalist ones.

## How to actually start

If you are starting from zero — either because you have nothing, or because the wardrobe you have is not working — the order matters.

Buy the coat first. It is the most expensive item, but it is the item that makes everything else work. With a good coat, the same jeans and sweater that look like laundry look like an outfit. The coat does the heavy lifting.

Buy the jeans second. The jeans you wear every day should fit you, not the model in the photos. Try on at least five pairs before committing. The fit is more important than the brand.

Buy the white shirts third. They will replace themselves. The first few will not last. The versions you settle on will last for years.

Buy the sweater, the blazer, the loafers in whatever order feels right. The order is less important than the conviction that you are not going to be doing this again in six months.

Buy the slip dress, the belt, the trench coat last. These are the items that complete the wardrobe rather than start it.

## The honest summary

A wardrobe of timeless pieces is not a fashion statement. It is a quiet refusal to participate in the trend cycle, which is something the trend cycle would prefer you not do. The pieces on this list will not make you look fashionable. They will make you look like you have not thought about what you are wearing, which is the most expensive-looking thing in the world.

The cost, in money and time, is real. The savings, in years of not thinking about clothes, is larger. The math is not complicated.

Buy one or two pieces a year from this list. In five years you will have a wardrobe that outlasts any trend and explains itself without language.

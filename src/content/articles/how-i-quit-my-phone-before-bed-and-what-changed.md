I charged my phone in the kitchen for two months as an experiment. I had read enough about the practical reasons to keep the phone out of the bedroom that I wanted to test them. The reasons were well-documented: better sleep, less anxiety, more morning productivity, better relationship with the screen. The reasons turned out to be correct. The change was not subtle. The experiment became a permanent rule.

This is what changed, what was hard, the system I have built to sustain the rule, and the small exceptions I make for the cases where the phone is genuinely needed.

## What changed

The changes were in three categories: sleep, mornings, and evenings.

**Sleep.** The most immediate change. The first night I slept without the phone in the bedroom, I fell asleep faster and woke up less during the night. The improvement was noticeable within a week. By the end of the second month, the improvement was significant — I was falling asleep about 20 minutes faster and waking up about 30 minutes less during the night.

The mechanism, as best I understand it, is that the phone in the bedroom creates a state of low-level arousal. The notification check (even if there are no notifications) creates a small cognitive event. The phone's glow (even on dim) creates a small light event. The phone's presence (even face-down) creates a small temptation event. The cumulative effect is a measurable degradation in sleep quality.

**Mornings.** The most striking change. Before the experiment, my first action of the day was reaching for the phone. The first 15 minutes of my morning were spent scrolling email, social media, news, and notifications. After the experiment, my first action of the day was getting out of bed, walking to the kitchen, and starting the day. The 15 minutes that used to be screen time became real morning time.

The change in my mornings was bigger than I expected. The 15 minutes of morning scrolling was not quality time. The 15 minutes was reactive, fragmented, and unproductive. Replacing it with real time (coffee, breakfast, thinking, reading) was a meaningful upgrade.

**Evenings.** The change I appreciated most. Before the experiment, my evenings ended in bed, scrolling. The phone-in-bed was the last hour of the day. The scrolling was usually social media, news, or email. The scrolling was not enjoyable. The scrolling was just the default.

After the experiment, my evenings ended in the living room or kitchen, with a book, or with my partner, or with a podcast. The phone was in the kitchen, charging. The evening had a defined ending. The ending was not the phone.

The phone-in-bedroom was making my evenings worse, not better. Removing the phone made my evenings better.

## What was hard

The first week was uncomfortable. The discomfort was not about the sleep. The discomfort was about the loss of the habit.

**The reflex to reach for the phone.** The most common difficulty. When I was lying in bed and wanted something to do, the reflex was to reach for the phone. Without the phone, the reflex had nowhere to go. The reflex had to be redirected. The redirection was the work of the first week.

**The fear of missing something.** The second difficulty. The fear was that something important would happen overnight, and I would miss it because the phone was not in the bedroom. The fear was largely unfounded. The emergencies that happen overnight are rare. The non-emergencies (emails, social media notifications, news) do not need an overnight response.

**The boredom of the bedroom.** The third difficulty. The bedroom, without the phone, was just a room. The room was for sleeping. The room was not for entertainment. The shift from "bedroom as entertainment space" to "bedroom as sleep space" was a meaningful reframe.

**The partner's reaction.** A fourth difficulty, specific to my situation. My partner did not initially do the experiment. The partner was charging the phone in the bedroom. The partner's phone glow was an additional distraction. The fix was to have the conversation and align on the rule. The conversation was not easy. The conversation was necessary.

## The system

The system that has lasted for three years. The system is not strict. The system has small exceptions. The system is sustainable because it is not over-engineered.

**The phone charges in the kitchen.** The rule. The rule has one location exception (when traveling). The rule is non-negotiable.

**An alarm clock replaces the phone alarm.** The replacement. The alarm clock is a simple analog clock with a battery backup. The cost was about €20. The alarm clock does not need to be charged. The alarm clock does not glow. The alarm clock does not have notifications.

**A book or e-reader replaces the bedtime scroll.** The alternative. The book is more relaxing than the phone. The book does not have notifications. The book is easier to put down. The book has a defined ending. The book is the right kind of entertainment for the bedroom.

**A podcast or audiobook is the alternative for travel.** The exception. When traveling, especially for work, I do listen to something to fall asleep. The podcast or audiobook is on the e-reader, not the phone. The e-reader has no notifications. The e-reader has no social media.

**The phone is on Do Not Disturb from 9 p.m. until 7 a.m.** The safety net. The DND is set to allow calls from favorites (in case of emergency) and to silence everything else. The DND is the protection against the rare emergency that needs an overnight response.

## What the system replaces

The system replaces a set of behaviors that were making my evenings and mornings worse.

**The reflexive scrolling.** The scrolling was not enjoyable. The scrolling was a habit. The scrolling was filling time that could have been spent doing something else. The scrolling is replaced by reading, by thinking, by conversation, by silence.

**The notification anxiety.** The notifications created a low-level anxiety. The anxiety was not about specific notifications. The anxiety was about the possibility of notifications. Removing the phone from the bedroom removes the possibility. The anxiety goes away.

**The blue light exposure.** The blue light from the phone was suppressing melatonin. The suppression was reducing sleep quality. Removing the phone from the bedroom removes the blue light. The melatonin is produced normally.

**The morning reactivity.** The morning scroll was reactive. The first 15 minutes of my morning were spent responding to other people's demands. The scroll was not a choice — it was a reaction. Replacing the morning scroll with real morning time was a choice.

## The exceptions

The cases where I make exceptions.

**Travel, when the phone is needed for navigation, boarding passes, or communication.** When traveling, the phone is the tool. The phone is in the hotel room. The phone is in my bag. The phone is not on the bedside table. The exceptions are minor.

**Emergencies.** When there is a known emergency (a sick family member, an ongoing work crisis), the phone is in the bedroom. The phone is on silent, not vibrate. The phone is checked when I wake up. The emergency ends. The rule returns.

**Specific work situations.** When I am on-call or on a deadline, the phone is in the bedroom. The phone is on silent. The phone is checked when I wake up. The situation ends. The rule returns.

The exceptions are exceptions. The exceptions are not the default. The default is the rule. The rule is the system.

## What I would say to someone considering the experiment

The experiment is worth trying. The setup is small (an alarm clock, a DND schedule, a book). The cost is small (about €20 for the clock). The benefit is large (better sleep, better mornings, better evenings).

The first week will be uncomfortable. The discomfort is the loss of the habit. The habit will be replaced by another habit (reading, conversation, thinking) within a week. The replacement habit is better than the original.

The system is sustainable. The system has lasted for three years. The system has not been hard to maintain. The system is one of the easiest changes I have made to my life with one of the largest returns.

The phone is a tool. The tool is useful during the day. The tool is not useful in the bedroom. The bedroom is for sleeping. The bedroom is for the people in the bedroom. The bedroom is not for the phone.

## The honest summary

Charging the phone in the kitchen changed my sleep, my mornings, and my evenings. The change was noticeable within a week. The change was significant within a month. The change has lasted for three years.

The system is simple: phone charges in the kitchen, alarm clock replaces the phone alarm, book replaces the bedtime scroll. The exceptions are minor. The exceptions are not the default.

The phone is the most useful tool in my life. The phone is also the most intrusive tool in my life. Removing the phone from the bedroom removes the intrusion. The intrusion was costing me sleep, mornings, and evenings.

If you are considering the experiment, try it for two months. The cost is small. The benefit is large. The system is sustainable. The system is one of the better ones you can adopt.

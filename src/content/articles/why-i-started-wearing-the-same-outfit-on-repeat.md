I have been wearing the same five outfits on rotation for two years. Two pairs of jeans (raw denim and washed denim). Three knitwear pieces (camel crewneck, navy roll-neck, grey cardigan). Two coats (the navy wool overcoat and the khaki trench). A white Oxford shirt. A grey t-shirt. The same brown derbies, the same white sneakers, the same black loafers.

The total number of garments in my work rotation is fifteen. The number of distinct outfits I can make from them is somewhere north of fifty. The number of outfits I actually wear is closer to twelve. The number I default to is five.

This is not a capsule wardrobe. It is not a uniform. It is the result of a slow drift toward wearing what works and stopping the rest. The drift started when I noticed that the morning decision about what to wear was using more cognitive energy than the morning decision about what to write, what to eat, or what to do with the day. The morning was supposed to be for thinking. The morning had become a costume fitting.

## What changed

Before the rotation, my closet had about 90 garments. I wore roughly 30 of them with any frequency. I had opinions about which ones went with which. I had rules about not wearing the same piece twice in a week. I had seasons in my closet, I had occasion-specific pieces, I had "good" pieces and "everyday" pieces. The system worked, in the sense that I was always dressed appropriately. The system also cost me about 15 minutes every morning, plus the cognitive overhead of tracking which pieces I had worn recently, which pieces needed washing, and which combinations I had worn in the last month.

The rotation removed most of that overhead. The morning decision is now binary: which jeans (one of two), which knitwear (one of three), which shoes (one of three). The number of distinct outfits is small enough that I have memorized all of them. The morning decision takes about 90 seconds. The cognitive overhead is essentially zero.

The visible result is that I look essentially the same most days. People have noticed. They have asked if I am going through something. They have asked if I lost weight (I did not). They have assumed I am not interested in clothing. None of these are true. I am interested in clothing. I am just less interested in the daily decision.

## What I kept and what I let go

The pieces in the rotation were not chosen on a single day. They were chosen over a slow culling process that took about a year. The method: I wore what I wanted to wear, and noticed which pieces I reached for repeatedly.

**Kept: the pieces I reached for even when no one was watching.** The camel crewneck because it goes with everything and feels right in the morning. The raw denim because it is the only pair of jeans I have ever owned that has held its shape after three years. The Oxford shirt because I have worn similar ones since I was 18 and they work in every context.

**Let go: the pieces I owned because they were impressive.** The jacket I bought because a magazine told me to. The shoes that required breaking in for two months. The sweater that was a beautiful color but felt wrong every time I wore it. The coat that is technically perfect but only works with one specific type of trouser. The pieces that justified their existence on paper but failed the daily-wear test.

The culling was not a single event. It was a year of noticing what I actually wore and letting the rest go. Some went to resale. Some went to charity. Some went to friends. The closet went from 90 pieces to about 40, and then from 40 to about 25. The 25 pieces are the ones I wear. The other 15 are the ones I keep for specific occasions (a wedding, a funeral, a black-tie dinner).

## What is missing

The rotation has costs. The most obvious is that I wear the same things. People notice. Some people find it odd. Some find it a little sad. The reaction is usually well-meaning but reflects a different theory of clothing than mine.

The theory most people hold is that clothing is a form of self-expression. Each outfit says something. Variety says something interesting. Repetition says something boring. Under this theory, my rotation is a failure: I am saying the same thing, every day, for years.

The theory I have come to hold is that clothing is a tool for being appropriately dressed while doing other things. The thing being expressed by the outfit is that I have somewhere to be. The variety of the outfit is not the point. The appropriateness of the outfit is the point. Under this theory, my rotation is a success: I am always appropriately dressed, I never spend more than 90 seconds thinking about it, and the cognitive energy I used to spend on clothing is now spent on things I care about more.

Neither theory is wrong. The question is which theory you want your closet to serve.

## The exceptions

The rotation is not a uniform. There are exceptions.

**Travel.** I pack differently for trips. A week in a city I have not been to requires different pieces than a week in a city I have. I still pack lightly, but the rotation breaks.

**Special occasions.** A wedding. A funeral. A black-tie event. A job interview. These call for specific pieces that are not in the rotation. I own these pieces, but they live in a different part of the closet.

**Weather extremes.** A heat wave or a cold snap requires pieces that are not in the rotation. A heavy winter coat, a winter hat, gloves. The rotation is for the temperate days, which are most days.

**Mood.** Occasionally I want to wear something specific — a vintage piece, a piece someone gave me, a piece I have not worn in a while. The rotation is the default. The exceptions are occasional.

The exceptions are not contradictions. They are part of the system. The system has a default. The default is the rotation. The exceptions are the days the rotation does not apply.

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from the rotation is that the closet is supposed to serve me, not the other way around. For most of my twenties and thirties, the closet was a project. The project was "develop a personal style." The project required attention. The attention was mostly spent on what to wear in the morning. The morning attention is the most expensive cognitive resource of the day, and I was spending it on clothing.

The rotation freed that resource. The morning is now for thinking about what to write, what to read, what to do with the day. The clothing is handled. The clothing is not the point.

The second lesson is that personal style does not require variety. Personal style is the recognizable signature in what you wear. A signature can be repeated. Steve Jobs's signature was the black turtleneck. Mark Zuckerberg's is the grey t-shirt. My signature, such as it is, is the camel crewneck and the raw denim. The signature is recognizable. The signature does not require variation.

The third lesson is that clothing is not the only form of self-expression. The expression that matters more to me now is in what I write, what I make, what I build, what I say, who I spend time with. The clothing is the wrapper. The wrapper does not need to be interesting. The thing inside the wrapper does.

## How to start

The transition is gradual. I did not throw out 65 pieces overnight. I threw out the pieces that were already not being worn. Then I watched what I reached for over the next six months. Then I threw out the pieces that I was still not reaching for. The rotation emerged from the culling.

If you want to try it, the method is simple. For the next three months, wear what you want. Do not impose any rules. Notice which pieces you reach for. Notice which pieces you avoid. The pieces you reach for are the rotation. The pieces you avoid are the candidates for letting go.

The culling will follow. The rotation will emerge. The morning decision will get shorter. The cognitive overhead will decrease. The closet will get smaller. The mornings will get better.

## The honest summary

I am not Steve Jobs. I am not Mark Zuckerberg. I am a person who wears clothes and cares about how they look and has slowly realized that the daily decision about clothing is not the most important decision of the day.

The rotation is not a moral position. It is a tool. The tool works for me. It might not work for you. The test is simple: are you spending more time thinking about what to wear than you want to spend? If yes, the rotation is worth trying. If no, your current system is fine.

The rotation is not the only way. There are other systems — capsule wardrobes, uniform dressers, slow-fashion enthusiasts, maximalists — that work for other people. The point is not to adopt my system. The point is to recognize that the system you have is a choice, and that the choice can be changed if the cost is too high.

Wear what works. Stop wearing what does not. The rotation is the result.

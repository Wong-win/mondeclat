Wearable health tracking is one of the most over-marketed and under-thought-about consumer categories. The pitch is "know your body." The reality is that most people who use wearables end up with more data than they can interpret, more notifications than they want, and a more anxious relationship with their own body than they had before.

Used well, wearables are quietly transformative. Used badly, they are a small daily tax on attention and self-image. The difference is intentionality.

## What the data actually tells you

The honest assessment of what consumer wearables can measure reliably in 2026:

**Well measured.** Steps, distance, heart rate (especially resting heart rate), sleep duration, basic SpO2, VO2 max estimate on most major brands, ECG on Apple Watch and some Fitbits.

**Roughly measured.** Calorie burn (off by 20 to 40 percent), sleep stages (REM/deep/light are estimates from heart rate and motion, not EEG), stress scores (proprietary and inconsistent), "readiness" metrics (varies wildly between brands).

**Not measured at all.** Hormones, glucose (without a continuous glucose monitor), true recovery, training load, nutritional status. Any wearable claiming to track these is marketing.

The mistake most people make is treating the second and third categories as if they were the first. They are not. They are directionally interesting and individually noisy.

## The setup that helps

### One device, one app

The single biggest mistake is having two wearables (a watch and a ring, for example) and reconciling the data manually. The two will not agree on calories, sleep stages, or readiness. You will spend mental energy arbitrating between them.

Pick one. Wear it. Trust it for what it measures well. Ignore the rest.

The current best options:
- **Apple Watch** for iPhone users who want a full smartwatch
- **Oura Ring** for people who want sleep and recovery tracking without a screen
- **Garmin** for serious training (running, cycling, swimming)
- **Whoop** for athletes who want load and strain metrics

### Pick three numbers

After a month of having a wearable, almost everyone tries to track everything. The result is information overload and decision paralysis.

Pick three numbers that map to actual decisions. Examples:

- Resting heart rate (lower is generally better; trends over weeks matter)
- Sleep duration (target 7 to 9 hours; consistency matters more than absolute hours)
- Weekly cardio minutes (target 150 minutes moderate or 75 minutes vigorous, per WHO)

Track those three. Ignore the rest. Make one small change based on what you see.

### Turn off most notifications

The default wearable notification setup is designed to maximize engagement, not minimize anxiety. Every ring, buzz, and achievement notification is a small interruption that pulls attention back to the device.

Turn off:
- Standing reminders (every hour is too frequent)
- Achievement notifications (you already know you closed a ring)
- Real-time stress alerts (do not need your watch telling you that you are stressed)
- Most app notifications

Keep on:
- Incoming call and message alerts (if useful)
- Heart-rate alerts for very high or very low rates
- Fall detection (if you are over 60)

### Weekly review, not daily check

The mental model that makes wearables useful is the weekly review. Once a week, glance at the three numbers above. Note any trends. Adjust one thing if needed.

The mental model that makes wearables harmful is the daily check. Pulling up the app three times a day to see how last night rated generates anxiety without producing action.

## What to skip

### Calorie burn as a budgeting tool

Wearable calorie burn estimates are accurate enough to be directionally interesting and inaccurate enough to be misleading as a basis for eating decisions. If you want to manage weight, track food intake (MyFitnessPal, Cronometer) and trust the food log, not the wearable.

### Stress scores as a diagnostic

Proprietary stress scores vary by brand, by day, by what you ate for breakfast. They are useful only as a long-term trend signal. Treating a single day's "high stress" reading as meaningful is a recipe for unnecessary worry.

### Readiness to train

Readiness scores are derived from the same unreliable inputs (HRV, sleep stages, recent load). They are useful as one input among many. They are not useful as a binary "should I train today" decision. Listen to your body. The score is a suggestion, not a verdict.

### Sleep scoring

Sleep stage accuracy on consumer wearables is around 60 to 70 percent against polysomnography, which is the clinical standard. The duration and consistency measures are accurate. The "deep sleep" minutes are an estimate. Treating the latter as gospel will make you anxious about something you cannot change.

## When wearables actually help

The strongest evidence for wearables is in:

- **Cardiac alerts.** Apple Watch and Fitbit have both detected atrial fibrillation in users before they had symptoms. This is real clinical value.
- **Activity maintenance.** People who wear a fitness tracker walk more than people who do not. The accountability effect is real.
- **Sleep consistency.** Seeing your bedtime drift on a chart is often the motivation to fix it.
- **Training load for athletes.** Garmin and Whoop provide useful inputs for serious runners and cyclists.

The common thread: wearables help most when they measure something objective and either alert to a problem or track a trend you can act on. They help least when they generate scores about subjective states (stress, readiness, recovery) that you cannot act on without becoming more anxious.

## The honest summary

A wearable is a tool, not a coach. The data it produces is information, not advice. The trends it shows are signals, not verdicts.

The people who get the most out of wearables treat them as a low-effort way to maintain a small set of habits — walk more, sleep enough, notice when something is off. The people who get the least are the ones who try to optimize every metric and end up optimizing nothing.

Wear the device, pick three numbers, review weekly, and ignore the rest. That is the setup that works. Everything else is a tax on attention you cannot afford.

## What I actually track and what I have stopped tracking

I have been using wearables since 2017. The first was a Fitbit Charge. The current one is an Apple Watch. In between, I tried Garmin, Whoop, and Oura. The setup I have now is what survived the experiments.

The three numbers I track:

**Resting heart rate.** This is the most reliable indicator of cardiovascular fitness that a consumer wearable can measure. Lower is generally better. Mine is 52 bpm, down from 65 when I started. The trend over months matters. The single-day reading does not.

**Sleep duration.** I track the duration, not the stages. The duration is reliable. The stages are estimates. The sleep I get is roughly 7 hours and 15 minutes per night, with weekend variance. The weekend variance is what I try to minimize.

**Weekly cardio minutes.** I track the minutes of moderate-to-vigorous cardio per week, not the calorie burn. The target is 150 minutes moderate or 75 minutes vigorous, per WHO. I usually hit 200 minutes. The number is the input to whether I need to add a workout.

The three numbers I stopped tracking:

**Calorie burn.** The numbers are off by 20 to 40 percent. The decisions I would make based on the number are wrong. I track food intake manually instead. The wearable calorie number is decorative.

**Sleep stages.** The deep sleep and REM estimates are directionally interesting and individually noisy. I am not going to change my behavior based on the noise. The duration is what matters.

**Stress scores.** Proprietary, inconsistent, useless. The subjective experience of stress is what I respond to, not the proprietary score. The score is a feature of the algorithm, not a feature of me.

**Readiness scores.** Same problem as stress scores. The score is derived from the same noisy inputs. The score is a suggestion, not a verdict. I listen to my body. The body is more reliable.

**Stand reminders.** I turned them off after the first month. The hourly reminder was more annoying than useful. The activity I needed to track was already tracked.

## The setup that has worked for me

After nine years of trying different setups, the one that has stuck:

**One device.** Apple Watch, worn continuously except for charging. The watch is on my left wrist. The right wrist is unworn. The single-device discipline has prevented the reconciliation problem of having two sources.

**Three numbers reviewed weekly.** Sunday evening, after the morning routine, I open the Health app and check the three numbers. The check takes about 90 seconds. The check is not a deep dive. The check is a trend assessment.

**One small change per week.** If one of the three numbers is off-trend, I make one small change. The change is usually about sleep (going to bed 15 minutes earlier) or cardio (adding a 20-minute walk). The change is small enough to be sustainable.

**No notifications during the day.** The watch does not buzz. The watch does not push activity reminders. The watch does not show me my heart rate unless I open it. The watch is a tool I consult, not a thing that interrupts me.

**Annual review.** Once a year, I review whether the device is still earning its place. The annual review is a 30-minute conversation with myself about whether the data is producing decisions. If the data is not producing decisions, I consider stopping the subscription.

## The categories I would consider in 2026

The wearable category has consolidated in 2026. The choices are clearer than they were in 2018.

**For most people.** Apple Watch (iPhone) or Fitbit (cross-platform). The choice depends on phone preference. Both have the same core functionality. The Apple Watch has better third-party app support. The Fitbit has better battery life.

**For serious athletes.** Garmin. The Garmin ecosystem is the most developed for running, cycling, swimming, and triathlon. The training load metrics are the most useful. The price is higher.

**For sleep and recovery focus.** Oura Ring. The ring form factor is good for sleep tracking because it does not have the display that causes evening engagement. The data is good. The subscription is required.

**For load and strain focus.** Whoop. The strain metric is unique to Whoop. The accuracy is debatable. The subscription is the entire business model.

The wrong choice is to buy more than one. The wrong choice is to wear a watch and a ring simultaneously. The two will not agree. The reconciliation is wasted time.

## What I have changed my mind about

I have been using wearables for nine years. My positions have shifted.

**I used to recommend fitness trackers as good for everyone.** I now think they are good for some people and bad for others. If you are the kind of person who gets anxious about numbers, do not use one. The anxiety cost is higher than the accountability benefit.

**I used to dismiss smartwatches as overpriced.** I now think the Apple Watch is worth the price for iPhone users who want a smartwatch. The integration with the iPhone is the value. The fitness features are bonus.

**I used to recommend tracking everything.** I now recommend tracking three numbers. The simplification is what makes the tracking sustainable.

**I used to say the data was objective.** The data is mostly objective for things like heart rate and steps. The data is mostly subjective for things like stress and sleep stages. The distinction is important.

**I used to think wearables were essential for health.** I now think they are useful for some specific things (cardiac alerts, activity maintenance) and useless for others (stress, readiness). The choice depends on which specific things matter to you.

**I used to recommend daily check-ins.** I now recommend weekly check-ins. The daily check-in is too frequent. The weekly check-in is the right cadence for most of the data.

## What I would tell someone considering a wearable

A short set of recommendations, based on what I have learned.

**If you are not currently active.** Do not buy a wearable. The accountability benefit is real but small. The cost is not justified. Build the habit first. Add the wearable when the habit is established.

**If you are currently active and want to track.** Buy the device that matches your phone. Use it for three months. Pick three numbers. Review weekly. If the setup is not producing decisions after three months, stop using it.

**If you have a specific health goal.** Use the wearable as a tool for that goal. The goal might be training for a race, recovering from an injury, or improving sleep. The wearable is useful for specific goals. It is less useful for vague "wellness."

**If you have an anxiety disorder.** Be cautious. The data can become a source of anxiety. The wearable is a tool. The tool can be misused. If the data is making you more anxious, stop using the device.

**If you are an athlete.** Use a sport-specific device. The Garmin, Polar, or Suunto ecosystems are more developed for serious training than the Apple Watch or Fitbit.

## The honest summary

Wearables are useful tools for specific use cases. The use cases that work are activity maintenance, sleep consistency, training load for athletes, and cardiac alerts. The use cases that do not work are stress management, weight management, and "wellness" generally.

The setup that works is one device, three numbers, weekly review, and minimal notifications. The setup that does not work is multiple devices, dozens of numbers, daily check-ins, and constant notifications. The first setup produces decisions. The second produces anxiety.

The category has matured. The 2026 devices are better than the 2017 devices. The decisions about whether to use one have not changed. The decision is whether the device will produce decisions for you. If yes, use one. If no, do not.

The cost is €200 to €500 for the device, plus optional subscriptions. The benefit is the specific decisions the data enables. The compounding effect is the habit maintenance the device supports. The cost is worth it for the specific use cases. The cost is not worth it for the vague use cases.

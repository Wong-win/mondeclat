My mother has not bought new clothes in fifteen years. That is not an exaggeration. Her wardrobe, which fills an entire wall of her bedroom, is a museum of the late 1990s and early 2000s — silk blouses from Jones New York, trouser suits from Talbots, a clutch from a wedding she attended in 2003 that she has never opened since.

She wears, on any given day, a navy cardigan over a white shirt and grey trousers. She has worn some version of this outfit five days a week for as long as I can remember. The rest of the wardrobe sits.

Last winter I went home for two weeks and helped her edit it. It was the most emotionally complicated thing I have ever done with her, and we are both better for it. This is what happened.

## The starting inventory

The wardrobe contained approximately three hundred pieces when we started. By the end, it contained one hundred and forty-seven. The discarded hundred and fifty-three were donated to a women's shelter, which felt right. They were not bad clothes. They were just not her clothes.

The categories we got rid of, in order of volume:

**Silk blouses in colors she does not wear.** Twenty-two. All bought between 1998 and 2005. None worn in the last decade. She had been keeping them because they were "good blouses." They were. They were also blouses for a different woman.

**The trouser suits.** Eight. From a period when she thought she needed to dress more formally for work than she actually did. None had been worn since retirement.

**"Going out" clothes.** A category that included sequined tops, cocktail dresses, and shoes she had worn to weddings of people she no longer speaks to. Thirty-eight pieces.

**Items from her previous life stage.** She had been thinner, then heavier, then thinner again. The clothes from each stage had been kept "in case." They had not been opened since the relevant stage ended. Twenty-six pieces.

**The "almost right" pile.** The largest category by far. Clothes that fit, mostly, but not quite. The colour was off. The cut was dated. The fabric was wrong for the climate she actually lives in. Forty-one pieces.

**The sentimental items.** Nine pieces that had real emotional attachment — her mother's wedding coat, a sweater her sister had knitted, my first school uniform. These we did not sort. These stay forever, regardless of fit or relevance.

## The questions that did the work

The actual sorting took three afternoons. The framework was five questions per item:

1. Have you worn this in the last twelve months?
2. If not, is there a specific occasion in the next twelve months when you would wear it?
3. Does it fit your life now, or a life you used to have?
4. If a stranger showed you this in a shop tomorrow, would you buy it?
5. If you keep it, where will it live, and what will you wear it with?

The first question eliminated sixty pieces on its own. The second caught most of the rest. The third was the one that made her cry, once, in the middle of a Wednesday afternoon.

The fourth was the question she had never been asked. It reframed her wardrobe as a series of decisions she would make now, not decisions she had made in 1999. The fifth was the operational question that turned "I might wear it someday" into "I would wear it with what?"

## What stayed

The wardrobe that emerged is one I would not have predicted. The keepers were not the expensive items, the new items, or the items with the most sentimental weight. They were the ones she had actually been wearing.

The cardigans. Eleven. She has worn cardigans every day of her adult life and she is not stopping now. Most are from J.Crew and L.L.Bean, bought between 2008 and 2019, in navy, grey, beige, and one burgundy. They fit her life. They go with everything she actually wears.

The trousers. Four pairs. Two grey flannel, one navy, one black. All from Talbots, all bought between 2005 and 2015, all altered at the waist. She has worn trousers five days a week for thirty years.

The shirts. Twenty-three. Almost all white or pale blue. Cotton, mostly. A few in linen. She buys these new when the old ones wear out and replace them with the same thing.

The shoes. Eight pairs. Two pairs of black loafers (one for daily, one for the garden), two pairs of walking shoes (one summer, one winter), one pair of dress shoes, one pair of boots, one pair of slippers, one pair of "nice" shoes she wears to funerals.

The coats. Four. A navy wool overcoat (twenty years old, still in rotation), a quilted jacket, a raincoat, and a winter parka.

The accessories. Three scarves (all silk, all neutral), one belt, three bags (one daily, one church, one travel).

That is seventy-four pieces. Plus the nine sentimental items she will never wear and never get rid of. Plus a small section of "guest" clothes — the things she keeps for when my sister visits with her children, who sometimes need a sweater.

Total: one hundred and forty-seven. Down from three hundred.

## What changed

The wardrobe is not the point. What changed is my mother's relationship to her clothes.

Before, the wardrobe was a source of vague, ambient guilt. Three hundred pieces she was not wearing represented three hundred decisions she had not been able to make. Every morning involved opening the closet, considering ten options, defaulting to the navy cardigan, and feeling quietly guilty about the other two hundred ninety-nine.

After, the wardrobe is a tool. She opens the closet, picks from a small selection that all fits her life, and the decision is over in thirty seconds. The guilt is gone. The decision fatigue is gone. The "what should I wear" anxiety that I had not realized was part of her morning routine is gone.

She has not bought a new piece of clothing since we finished. She has worn the navy cardigan five days a week, the same as always, and she is more comfortable in it than she has been in years.

## Why this is a feminist act

My mother is a woman who spent forty years making decisions about other people's clothes — for her children, for her husband, for her workplace — and never learned to make them for herself. She bought what she thought she was supposed to buy. She kept what she thought she was supposed to keep. She never asked herself whether she actually wanted any of it.

The edit was the first time anyone asked her.

That is not a small thing. The wardrobe she had was a wardrobe she had been talked into by every retail environment she had walked through in forty years. The wardrobe she has now is one she has chosen, even if the choice was mostly to keep what she already wears.

The feminist case is not that she threw things away. It is that she got to decide.

## What I learned

A few things I will carry into my own wardrobe.

First: most people are not wearing most of what they own. The empirical number from studies on closet utilization is around 20 percent. My mother's number was 25 percent. She was average.

Second: the clothes people keep are not the clothes they love. They are the clothes they feel obligated to keep. Sentiment, guilt, and the sunk-cost fallacy are doing more work in most wardrobes than taste.

Third: editing a wardrobe is not the same as shopping for one. The decisions that matter are the ones about what to let go. The decisions about what to acquire are downstream of those.

Fourth: this is emotional work, not practical work. The fact that my mother cried on a Wednesday afternoon tells you that the clothes were carrying weight I had not understood. A wardrobe is not just clothes. It is the visible record of how someone sees themselves.

Fifth: the slow-fashion argument applies to what you already own, not just to what you buy. The most sustainable wardrobe is the one you have already paid for and are actually wearing. Editing is more sustainable than any purchase decision.

## What I would tell someone else's mother

If you have a parent — particularly an aging parent — with a wardrobe that has gotten out of sync with their life, the offer to help them edit it is one of the most generous things you can do. Not because the outcome matters in any practical sense, but because the conversation that has to happen in order to get there is one most people will never have with their parents.

What do you actually wear? What do you actually like? What kind of life are you actually living?

These are questions most people only get asked once. For my mother, it took me thirty-eight years to ask.

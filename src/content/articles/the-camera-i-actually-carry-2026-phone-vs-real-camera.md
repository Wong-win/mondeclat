I have been a serious amateur photographer for ten years. The seriousness is what produced the debate. The debate was about whether to bring a real camera or just use the phone. The debate ended in 2025 when I realized the answer is neither. The answer is “both, for different things.”

Here is the setup, what I carry, and how I use each.

## The state of phone cameras in 2026

What phones can now do.

**Sensor size.** The iPhone 16 Pro Max, Samsung S25 Ultra, and Pixel 9 Pro have 1-inch or near-1-inch sensors. The sensor size is what was reserved for high-end compact cameras five years ago. The sensor is what determines image quality.

**Computational photography.** The phones use computational photography to combine multiple exposures into one image. The result is HDR, depth mapping, and noise reduction that no traditional camera can match. The match is what makes the phones excellent in challenging light.

**Multiple lenses.** The phones have ultra-wide, wide, and telephoto lenses. The lenses cover most focal lengths. The lengths are what most people need. The need is what the phones satisfy.

**Video.** The phones shoot 4K at 60fps, with excellent stabilization. The stabilization is what makes them the best video cameras for most people. The people are what the phones are for.

**Storage.** The phones have 256GB to 1TB of storage. The storage holds thousands of photos. The photos are what most people shoot.

The 2026 phone camera is excellent for most situations. The situations are what most people encounter. The encounter is what the phone handles.

## The state of dedicated cameras in 2026

What dedicated cameras still do better.

**Sensor size.** The APS-C and full-frame sensors in mirrorless cameras are still much larger than phone sensors. The size is what determines image quality in low light. The light is where phones struggle.

**Lens quality.** The dedicated lenses (Sony, Canon, Fuji, Sigma) have larger glass, faster apertures, and better optical design. The design is what produces the “look” that photographers chase. The look is what phones try to replicate but cannot match.

**Manual control.** The dedicated cameras have physical dials and buttons for shutter speed, aperture, ISO, and exposure compensation. The controls are what photographers need for full creative control. The control is what phones approximate but cannot match.

**RAW capture.** The dedicated cameras shoot 14-bit RAW files. The files contain all the data the sensor captured. The data is what allows extensive editing. The editing is what the files enable.

**Battery life.** The dedicated cameras shoot 500-1000 shots per battery. The phones die after a day of heavy shooting. The death is the issue. The issue is what dedicated cameras avoid.

**Form factor.** The dedicated cameras are designed for photography. The design is what makes them ergonomic for extended shooting. The shooting is what photographers do.

## What I actually carry

The honest answer.

**Always:** iPhone 16 Pro Max. The phone is what I have on me always. The phone is what I use for most photos. The phone is what produces most of my images. The images are the bulk of my photography.

**For specific occasions:** Sony A7C II with a 35mm f/1.8 lens. The camera is what I bring for specific situations: travel, portraits, low-light events, intentional photo walks. The situations are when I want the quality and control. The quality is what the camera provides.

**For hiking and outdoors:** Fujifilm X100VI. The X100VI is the best carry-everywhere camera. The camera has a fixed 35mm equivalent lens. The lens is what I shoot most with. The fixed lens is what makes me slow down and think about composition. The thinking is what the camera encourages.

**Never:** A DSLR. The DSLRs are dead to me. The weight and size are not worth the image quality gain over mirrorless. The mirrorless is what replaced them.

## How I decide which to use

The framework.

**Phone if:**

- The moment is fleeting and the phone is in my hand
- The light is good (daylight, well-lit indoor)
- I am not specifically going out to take photos
- The photo is for social media or casual sharing
- I want to be present in the moment, not behind a camera

**Real camera if:**

- I have planned a photo walk or trip
- The light is challenging (low light, high contrast)
- I want the best possible image quality
- I am shooting portraits or products
- I want the slower, more deliberate process

The decision is rarely binary. The decision is usually about which tool is more appropriate for the situation. The situation is what determines the tool. The tool is what I reach for.

## What each does best in 2026

The specific strengths.

**Phone excels at:**

- Spontaneous moments with people
- Quick social media content
- Video for vlogs or reels
- Low-light social media shots (with night mode)
- Travel snapshots when bringing a camera is impractical
- Photos for messaging and sharing

**Real camera excels at:**

- Portrait photography (especially with fast lenses)
- Landscape photography (especially at sunrise/sunset)
- Low-light photography (events, indoor without flash)
- Street photography (with manual controls)
- Long-exposure photography (waterfalls, stars)
- Photography as a practice (intentional, slow, deliberate)

The strengths are not overlapping. The strengths are what each tool is for. The for is what determines the choice.

## What I would recommend

If you are choosing.

**If you only buy one thing:** Buy the best phone you can afford. The phone is what you have on you. The phone is what produces most of your photos. The photos are what matter.

**If you are serious about photography:** Add a dedicated camera. The camera is what produces the images you cannot get from a phone. The images are what make the difference.

**If you want to learn photography:** Start with a phone. The phone is what you have. The learning is what the phone enables. The enablement is what the start is for.

**If you want to shoot more:** Carry a small dedicated camera always. The camera is what makes you think photographically. The thinking is what produces more photos. The more is the goal.

**If you want the best image quality:** Get a full-frame mirrorless with fast lenses. The full-frame is what produces the best quality. The quality is what you want.
